The source is a 1971 [National Aeronautics and Space Administration (NASA)] debriefing record concerning the [Apollo 14](/projects/apollo-14) crew’s observations of cosmic ray visual phenomena (light flashes). It records testimony from Stuart Roosa, [Alan Shepard](/people/alan-shepard) and [Edgar Mitchell](/people/edgar-mitchell), concentrating on the appearance, direction, timing and conditions of the flashes. Roosa said that most streaks appeared at the periphery of his vision,<sup>1</sup> while Shepard described the flashes as generally random in direction.<sup>2</sup> Mitchell reported a streak moving across his field of view.<sup>3</sup> The record also distinguishes between simple streaks or flashes and more expansive phenomena described by the crew using the term “nova”. Roosa characterised a nova as an eruption-like event,<sup>5</sup> while Mitchell described one as a blob that expanded and became more diffuse.<sup>6</sup>

A substantial part of the record concerns observation controls and quantitative reporting. According to the source record, the cabin lighting was completely dark during the formal observation period.<sup>7</sup> The crew’s reported counts were recorded by eye: Mitchell reported 12 flashes in his right eye and 6 in his left, while Shepard reported 10 and 4, and Roosa 6 and 2.<sup>8</sup> The source record describes the aggregate reports as biased towards the right eye by roughly three or four to one.<sup>9</sup> It also states that the crew had the impression that the flashes occurred within the eye rather than in the cabin.<sup>10</sup> The experiment included a flashlight demonstration: Mitchell reported that flashes returned approximately 30 to 40 seconds after the light was wiped across his eyes,<sup>11</sup> although approximately 17 minutes elapsed before the first flash was recorded during the formal session.<sup>12</sup> The testimony includes a single reported silver or blue-white flash resembling a blue diamond<sup>13</sup> and a separate diffuse, white or silver cloud-like phenomenon without a visible streak.<sup>14</sup> Its importance as a research source lies in this combination of individual descriptions, recorded counts and controlled-observation details; the debriefing team considered the resulting Apollo 14 data substantially improved over those from previous missions.<sup>15</sup>
