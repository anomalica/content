Clarke](/people/arthur-c-clarke) was known for creating visionary depictions of possible futures.<sup>1</sup> The record presents his remarks on possible life on Mars, reporting that he told SPACE.com he was fairly convinced that life had been discovered there.<sup>2</sup> It states that [Buzz Aldrin](/people/buzz-aldrin) was listening while Clarke made those remarks.<sup>3</sup> Clarke also said that photographs from the Jet Propulsion Laboratory (JPL) constituted convincing proof of large forms of life on Mars.<sup>4</sup> According to the record, Aldrin did not comment on that photographic claim and instead raised the subject of [zero-point energy](/topics/zero-point-energy).<sup>5</sup>

The source describes zero-point energy as an energy source theorised by some physicists to exist in the vacuum of space.<sup>6</sup> [Buzz Aldrin](/people/buzz-aldrin) said that zero-point energy would be needed to reach Alpha Centauri, and suggested that this might take 200 to 400 years.<sup>7</sup> He asked [Arthur C. Clarke](/people/arthur-c-clarke) to confirm agreement with that view.<sup>8</sup> Clarke said he was glad Aldrin had raised what he called the controversial question of zero-point energy.<sup>9</sup> He also said that many of his scientist friends considered belief in the idea crazy.<sup>10</sup> Clarke connected the idea of new forms of energy with cold fusion, which he said had been laughed out of court.<sup>11</sup> He nevertheless said he was convinced that new forms of energy were being tapped, making even nuclear energy look trivial in comparison.<sup>12</sup> Clarke further believed that controlling such energy would open up the universe.<sup>13</sup>

The record matters because it places claims about extraterrestrial life alongside speculation about propulsion and energy, while preserving disagreement and uncertainty within the exchange. It also records Clarke’s response to disbelief in extraordinary but well-documented events. He referred specifically to the Apollo program Moon missions as an example of events that some people refused to believe.<sup>14</sup> Clarke considered it inconceivable that a programme involving hundreds of thousands of people, conducted over more than a decade and amid extensive publicity, could have been faked.<sup>15</sup> In this respect, the source documents both the anomalous claim about life on Mars and the evidential standard Clarke applied to a conventional historical event.
