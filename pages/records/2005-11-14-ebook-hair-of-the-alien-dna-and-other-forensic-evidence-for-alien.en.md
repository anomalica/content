*Hair of the Alien: DNA and Other Forensic Evidence for Alien Abductions* is a 2005 source by [Bill Chalker](/people/bill-chalker) centred on biological and forensic approaches to alleged abduction reports. Chalker describes a hair sample as the book's central focus.<sup>1</sup> According to the account, [Peter Khoury](/people/peter-khoury) said that the sample was collected in Sydney in July 1992 after an apparent encounter with two almost-human females.<sup>2</sup> The investigation is presented as a standard forensic examination of the hair shaft, alongside control samples from Peter and Vivian Khoury.<sup>3</sup> The analytical target was mitochondrial hypervariable region I, spanning nucleotides 16,000 to 16,400.<sup>4</sup> A two-centimetre section of shaft above the root was excised for extraction.<sup>5</sup> Microscopy distinguished the sample from Peter Khoury's thick black hair and Vivian Khoury's normally thick brown hair; the questioned hair was described as extremely thin and almost clear, with little melanin.<sup>6</sup>

The book reports that analysis of the blond-hair sample produced five consistent substitutions relative to the human consensus, two of them described as rare.<sup>7</sup> A survey of mitochondrial-DNA literature identified four people carrying the same unusual substitution pattern; they were described as having Mongoloid Chinese ancestry and dark hair.<sup>8</sup> This created a discrepancy between the reported blond appearance and the genetic comparison. The Anomaly Physical Evidence Group (APEG) concluded that the results ruled out a fair-skinned, nearly albino woman from Sydney as the source, and considered dyeing and albinism inadequate explanations.<sup>9</sup> A second phase examined root tissue and reported a mitochondrial DNA type different from that obtained from the hard shaft.<sup>10</sup> The biochemists reported that they had no explanation for the presence of two mitochondrial DNA types in one piece of hair.<sup>11</sup> The source also records a limitation: the sample was eight years old and yielded relatively little mitochondrial DNA and almost no nuclear DNA.<sup>12</sup>

The case matters within the source because it illustrates both the potential and the evidential difficulties of applying forensic science to the alien abduction phenomenon. The discussion notes that greater sensitivity in DNA testing increases the importance of contamination and error controls.<sup>13</sup> Chalker therefore argues that unusual results should be tested against a substantial number of legitimate samples before validation.<sup>14</sup> He presents the findings as subject to continuing review and debate rather than as a settled conclusion.<sup>15</sup>
