**Journeys Out of the Body** is a first-person account by [Robert Monroe](/people/robert-monroe) of his reported Out-of-the-Body Experience (OOBE) and the ideas that emerged from it. According to the source, Monroe divided his research into four chronological stages,<sup>1</sup> beginning with the Beginning Stage of out-of-body research (Robert Monroe) from September 1958 to July 1959.<sup>2</sup> He said that he named the condition of leaving the physical body the Second State, and the non-physical body involved the Second Body.<sup>3</sup> The account is therefore both a record of experiences and an attempt to establish a vocabulary for describing them.

A central part of the source is Monroe's classification of reported environments. He identified Locale I as one of three Second State environments,<sup>4</sup> associated with people and places existing in the material world. He described Locale II as a non-material environment whose laws of motion and matter were only remotely related to those of the physical world,<sup>5</sup> and Locale III as an almost Earth-like physical-matter world with different technological and historical characteristics.<sup>6</sup> These distinctions matter because they separate reported observations intended to be checked against ordinary surroundings from more speculative accounts of unfamiliar settings, entities and post-mortem existence.

The book also documents efforts to examine the reports under controlled conditions. Charles T. Tart conducted laboratory studies with Monroe between September 1965 and August 1966,<sup>7</sup> during which Monroe produced two brief OOBEs on the eighth laboratory night.<sup>8</sup> Tart reported that Monroe correctly described the absence of the technician from the recording room and the presence of her husband in a corridor.<sup>9</sup> In the EEG Experiment #EEG-5 (1966-07-19), electrodes were attached while Monroe reclined on a cot,<sup>10</sup> and Tart later stated that the recording showed unusual tracings during the reported activity.<sup>11</sup> The source presents these observations alongside cautions about fear, which Monroe identified as a major obstacle,<sup>12</sup> and a return technique developed from hundreds of personal tests.<sup>13</sup> It thus remains significant as a detailed primary account linking anomalous experience, self-observation and early laboratory investigation, while also presenting broader speculation, including Monroe's laboratory animal hypothesis.<sup>14</sup>
