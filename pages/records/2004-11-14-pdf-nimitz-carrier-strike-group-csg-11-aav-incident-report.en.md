The Nimitz Carrier Strike Group (CSG-11) AAV Incident Report is a US Navy investigative account of the detection, intercept, and subsequent internal handling of Anomalous Aerial Vehicles (AAVs) encountered by Nimitz Carrier Strike Group (CSG-11) during pre-deployment training off the western coast of the United States. According to the report, the strike group operated in this area from approximately 2004-11-10 to 2004-11-16 in preparation for deployment to the Arabian Sea,<sup>1</sup> with the events themselves taking place during November and December 2004 in the USA, California, SOCAL Operating Area off the coast of California and Baja California Mexico.<sup>2</sup> The strike group comprised [USS Nimitz (CVN-68)](/objects/uss-nimitz-cvn-68), [USS Princeton (CG-59)](/objects/uss-princeton-cg-59), USS Chafee (DDG-90), USS Higgins (DDG-76), and USS Louisville (SSN-724).<sup>3</sup> Nimitz was home to Carrier Air Wing 11 (CVW-11),<sup>4</sup> which included [Marine Fighter Attack Squadron 232 (VMFA-232)](/organisations/marine-fighter-attack-squadron-232-vmfa-232), Strike Fighter Squadron 14 (VFA-14), [Strike Fighter Squadron 41 (VFA-41)](/organisations/strike-fighter-squadron-41-vfa-41), Strike Fighter Squadron 94 (VFA-94), Electronic Attack Squadron 135 (VAQ-135), Carrier Airborne Early Warning Squadron 117 (VAW-117), Helicopter Antisubmarine Squadron 6 (HS-6), and Fleet Logistics Support Squadron 30 Detachment 3 (VRC-30 Det 3).<sup>5</sup> The report states that the only participants in the detection and intercept events were Princeton, VAW-117, VMFA-232, and VFA-41.<sup>6</sup> Princeton, a Ticonderoga-class guided-missile cruiser<sup>7</sup> and the first of its class to carry the upgraded AN/SPY-1 radar,<sup>8</sup> was completing a Tailored Ships Training Availability (TSTA) during this period as part of the deployment work-up.<sup>9</sup>

According to the report, Princeton detected multiple AAVs operating in and around the strike group's vicinity on several occasions.<sup>2</sup> A Senior Chief aboard Princeton described the AAVs descending from very high altitude into the AN/SPY-1 scan volume at high velocity,<sup>10</sup> with the top of that scan volume placing the vehicles higher than approximately 18,000 metres.<sup>11</sup> One AAV was reported to descend from around 18,000 metres to around 15 metres in a matter of seconds,<sup>3</sup> hovering on radar for a short time before departing at high velocities and turn rates.<sup>4</sup> The report states the SPY-1 radar was set to an Air Intercept mode configured for conventional aircraft, and as a result never obtained an accurate track of the AAVs, repeatedly dropping the contacts as clutter.<sup>12</sup> Princeton's Tactical Air Officer reportedly could not identify the contact and was perplexed by its speed and altitude.<sup>13</sup> The AAV was detected by Princeton on three separate occasions during the week.<sup>14</sup>

On 2004-11-14, after again detecting an AAV, Princeton tasked two airborne F/A-18Fs for reconnaissance.<sup>15</sup> According to the report, the E-2C Hawkeye Air Control Officer did not see the AAV on his own radar until Princeton directed the contact,<sup>16</sup> and Princeton initially reported the target at approximately 4,600 to 6,100 metres mean sea level<sup>17</sup> before taking control of the F/A-18s directly, after which the E-2C remained airborne but uninvolved in the contact.<sup>18</sup> [David Fravor](/people/david-fravor), Commanding Officer of VFA-41, piloted the lead aircraft of the flight known as FastEagle 01,<sup>19</sup> with [Alex Dietrich](/people/alex-dietrich) piloting FastEagle 02.<sup>20</sup> Fravor's flight, reporting two captive training AIM-9M missiles as their only ordnance,<sup>21</sup> was vectored by Princeton (callsign "Poison") toward an unknown contact.<sup>22</sup> Fravor's crew first gained situational awareness via sporadic Link-16 tracks from an off-board sensor,<sup>23</sup> and his F/A-18F was not carrying a forward-looking infrared (FLIR) pod during the intercept.<sup>24</sup> Fravor's first indication of the contact was a visual disturbance on the water below the object, after which he gained visual on the object itself,<sup>25</sup> which he described as localised beneath the object rather than a trail or wake, resembling frothy waves and foam as if the water were boiling.<sup>26</sup> Fravor detached Dietrich's aircraft to hold at approximately 6,100 metres while his own F/A-18F descended to between approximately 3,700 and 4,900 metres,<sup>27</sup> and he attempted an unsuccessful "helmet lock" using the Joint Helmet Mounted Cuing System, which cues aircraft sensors to a pilot's line of sight and carries a recording capability<sup>28</sup> that Fravor said was rarely used, so he did not think to activate it.<sup>29</sup> Fravor said the object was "holding like a Harrier," referencing the AV-8B Harrier's hover and vertical/short takeoff and landing capability,<sup>30</sup> and described its shape as an elongated egg or "Tic Tac" with a discernable midline horizontal axis,<sup>31</sup> uniformly white across its entire body.<sup>32</sup> Beginning a descent from about 3,000 metres and approximately 650 kilometres per hour with the intention of a close aboard pass to visually identify the object,<sup>33</sup> Fravor assessed that the AAV appeared to recognise his aircraft, realigning its axis to point toward him.<sup>34</sup> As his flight completed the manoeuvre, the object ascended quickly and pulled a lift vector on and aft of them at supersonic speed.<sup>35</sup> Princeton subsequently reported that the AAV had flown to Fravor's flight's combat air patrol station, having climbed to approximately 7,300 metres,<sup>36</sup> but the flight found nothing on the surface or subsurface, with no indication of the earlier water disturbance.<sup>37</sup> A fellow F/A-18F pilot on the mission said all aircraft systems were functional throughout, with no interference and entry into the Link-16 network.<sup>38</sup>

Separately, the Commanding Officer of VMFA-232, flying an unarmed single-seat F/A-18C on a Functional Check Flight,<sup>39</sup> recalled being asked 30 minutes into his flight to investigate an unidentified contact, an unusual request,<sup>40</sup> and being asked whether he had ordnance onboard, which he found odd.<sup>41</sup> He gained radar contact of what he believed to be two F/A-18Fs approaching the AAV from the west at low altitude,<sup>42</sup> and as he descended through approximately 4,600 metres about 28 kilometres from the contact, he observed a water disturbance approximately 50 to 100 metres in diameter and close to round.<sup>43</sup> He never saw any object or vessel associated with the disturbance,<sup>44</sup> nor did he make visual contact with the other fighters or the AAV itself.<sup>45</sup> He recovered aboard Nimitz around 1200 local time<sup>46</sup> and was later asked at the Carrier Intelligence Center (CVIC) whether he had seen the "supersonic Tic Tac."<sup>47</sup>

Fravor's flight met with [Chad Underwood](/people/chad-underwood)'s flight in the paraloft aboard Nimitz and passed on what they had seen.<sup>48</sup> Underwood, the Weapon System Operator of VFA-41's second F/A-18F section airborne that day,<sup>49</sup> was asked by Fravor's crew to try to see and record what they could.<sup>50</sup> Underwood said it was a clear day with no clouds and a discernible blue horizon during his flight's FLIR tracking of the object,<sup>51</sup> which occurred at approximately 15:00 local time,<sup>52</sup> with initial radar tracks appearing approximately 55-74 kilometres to the south.<sup>53</sup> He attempted multiple times to transition the radar to Single Target Track mode, but it could not obtain a lock,<sup>54</sup> and he said there were no jamming cues or other normal electronic-attack indications present.<sup>55</sup> Underwood estimated the object to be co-altitude with his aircraft or a few thousand feet below, between approximately 4,600 and 6,100 metres.<sup>56</sup> The object appeared stationary on the FLIR display, with no discernible movement beyond closure from his own aircraft's motion,<sup>57</sup> and it was reported as solid white, smooth, with no edges, uniformly coloured with no nacelles, pylons, or wings.<sup>58</sup> As Underwood watched, the object moved out of the FLIR field of view; he made no attempt to slew the sensor and lost situational awareness of it.<sup>59</sup> He later said he could not confirm the object he tracked on FLIR was the same one described by Fravor's flight, since he never had visual contact himself.<sup>60</sup> Back aboard Nimitz, Underwood said CVIC personnel attempted to collect his tapes but he refused,<sup>61</sup> though copies were made and a set turned into the intelligence section.<sup>62</sup> He was not asked to sign any non-disclosure agreement and remained uncertain how far up the chain of command the reporting went.<sup>63</sup>

The report also addresses possible conventional explanations. A former submarine officer aboard USS Louisville said there were no unidentified sonar contacts in the vicinity of the aerial sightings,<sup>64</sup> and on this basis the report assessed it highly unlikely that the AAV operated below the ocean surface, while noting it remained possible the AAV was "cloaked" or invisible to the human eye given pilot reports of water disturbance with no visible craft.<sup>65</sup> Within the reporting chain, the Carrier Air Wing Intelligence Officer said that he and his colleagues initially did not take Fravor's report seriously, but had no reason to question it given that it came from an experienced and well-respected squadron commanding officer.<sup>66</sup> The report was passed to the Commander of CVW-11, a highly experienced aviator who also did not take it very seriously.<sup>67</sup> An email-based mission report, including the FLIR video, was subsequently sent via secure system to Third Fleet Intelligence (N2),<sup>68</sup> where the deputy N2 said it was read and acknowledged but not forwarded further up the chain of command.<sup>69</sup> The strike group was commanded at the time by Rear Admiral D.C. Curtis, later a Vice Admiral commanding Naval Surface Forces,<sup>70</sup> and the group's Director of Operations (N3) confirmed that, aside from the mission report, no other official report or statement was produced by the CSG regarding the encounter.<sup>71</sup> The events described form part of the broader [2004 USS Nimitz UAP encounter](/events/2004-uss-nimitz-uap-encounter), and are separately documented in relation to the [Anomalous Aerial Vehicle (AAV)](/topics/anomalous-aerial-vehicle-aav) more generally, alongside supporting systems such as the E-2C Hawkeye, AN/APS-145 Airborne Surveillance Radar, and [United States Navy](/organisations/united-states-navy-usn) reporting procedures referenced throughout the report.
