The Nimitz Carrier Strike Group (CSG-11) AAV Incident Report is an internal US Navy document recording the detection and intercept of an [Anomalous Aerial Vehicle (AAV)](/concepts/anomalous-aerial-vehicle-aav) by the [Nimitz Carrier Strike Group (CSG-11)](/organisations/nimitz-carrier-strike-group-csg-11) during November 2004. The strike group — comprising [USS Nimitz](/objects/uss-nimitz), [USS Princeton](/objects/uss-princeton), [USS Chafee](/objects/uss-chafee), [USS Higgins](/objects/uss-higgins), and [USS Louisville](/objects/uss-louisville)<sup>1</sup> — was operating in the [SOCAL Operating Area](/places/socal-operating-area) off the [Baja Mexico Coast](/places/baja-mexico-coast) during the period of approximately 2004-11-10 to 2004-11-16 in preparation for deployment to the Arabian Sea.<sup>2</sup> The report draws on accounts from radar operators, aircrew, and intelligence personnel, and was compiled in connection with the broader [2004 Nimitz UAP Incident](/matters/2004-nimitz-uap-incident).

USS Princeton, a Ticonderoga-class guided-missile cruiser and the first of its class to carry the upgraded [AN/SPY-1](/objects/anspy-1)B radar system,<sup>3</sup> detected multiple AAVs operating in and around the vicinity of the carrier strike group on several occasions during this period.<sup>4</sup> The [AN/SPY-1](/objects/anspy-1) is a multifunctional phased-array radar with a power output of 4 megawatts capable of tracking over 100 targets, designed for long-range volume search, fire-control-quality tracking, and [Ballistic Missile Defense](/concepts/ballistic-missile-defense).<sup>5</sup> AAV contacts were detected on three separate occasions during the week.<sup>6</sup> A Senior Chief aboard USS Princeton with 17 years of Aegis fire control experience assessed that the AAV exhibited ballistic missile characteristics in reference to its appearance, velocity, and radar indications.<sup>7</sup> The AN/SPY-1 radar was operating in a mode configured for conventional aircraft intercept and never obtained an accurate track on the AAVs; they were dropped by the computer as false targets.<sup>8</sup> The report notes that if the radar had been set in ballistic missile tracking mode, it likely would have had the capability to track the AAV.<sup>9</sup> USS Princeton was completing the [Tailored Ships Training Availability 2004](/events/tailored-ships-training-availability-2004) during the detection period.<sup>10</sup> The [Composite Training Unit Exercise (COMPTUX) Carrier Strike Group 11 (CSG-11) Training 2004](/events/composite-training-unit-exercise-comptux-carrier-strike-group-11-csg-11-training-2004) was also under way, and the USS Princeton Fire Control Officer and his technician initially believed the AAV contacts were part of that exercise.<sup>11</sup>

USS Nimitz was home to [Carrier Air Wing 11 (CVW-11)](/organisations/carrier-air-wing-11-cvw-11),<sup>12</sup> which included [Strike Fighter Squadron 41 (VFA-41)](/organisations/strike-fighter-squadron-41-vfa-41) flying the [F/A-18F](/objects/fa-18f), [Marine Fighter Attack Squadron 232 (VMFA-232)](/organisations/marine-fighter-attack-squadron-232-vmfa-232) flying the [F/A-18C](/objects/fa-18c), [Carrier Airborne Early Warning Squadron 117 (VAW-117)](/organisations/carrier-airborne-early-warning-squadron-117-vaw-117) flying the [E-2C Hawkeye](/objects/e-2c-hawkeye), [Electronic Attack Squadron 135 (VAQ-135)](/organisations/electronic-attack-squadron-135-vaq-135), [Helicopter Anti-Submarine Squadron 6 (HS-6)](/organisations/helicopter-anti-submarine-squadron-6-hs-6), [Fleet Logistics Support Squadron 30 (VRC-30) Det 3](/organisations/fleet-logistics-support-squadron-30-vrc-30-det-3), [Strike Fighter Squadron 14 (VFA-14)](/organisations/strike-fighter-squadron-14-vfa-14), and [Strike Fighter Squadron 94 (VFA-94)](/organisations/strike-fighter-squadron-94-vfa-94).<sup>13</sup> The report records that the only participants in the detection and intercept of the AAV were USS Princeton, VAW-117, VMFA-232, and VFA-41.<sup>14</sup> During the [Nimitz F/A-18F UAP Intercept, 2004-11-14](/events/2004-11-14-nimitz-fa-18f-uap-intercept), USS Princeton vectored two F/A-18Fs to intercept the AAV after again detecting it,<sup>15</sup> taking control of the aircraft from the E-2C Hawkeye for the duration of the contact and intercept.<sup>16</sup> The intercept took place at approximately 31°20'N, 117°10'W, approximately 130 kilometres south of the US/Mexico border and approximately 55 kilometres off the Baja Mexico Coast.<sup>17</sup>

[Commander David Fravor](/people/david-fravor), Commanding Officer of VFA-41, led the first intercept section as pilot of FASTEAGLE 01.<sup>18</sup> [Lieutenant Alex Dietrich](/people/alex-dietrich) piloted the second F/A-18F, FASTEAGLE 02.<sup>19</sup> Fravor's aircraft carried an [APG-73 Radar](/objects/apg-73-radar) in Range While Search mode at 150-kilometre scale<sup>20</sup> and no forward-looking infrared (FLIR) pod;<sup>21</sup> situational awareness to the AAV was initially received via sporadic [Link-16](/objects/link-16) tracks from an off-board sensor the Weapons and Sensors Officer (WSO) assumed was USS Princeton's AN/SPY-1.<sup>22</sup> Fravor's first visual indication was a [Water Disturbance](/objects/water-disturbance) below the AAV, which he described as localised underneath the object, appearing as frothy waves and foam almost as if the water was boiling.<sup>23</sup> He described the AAV as shaped like an elongated egg or 'Tic Tac', approximately 14 metres in length, with a discernible midline horizontal axis and uniformly white across the entire body.<sup>24</sup> The FASTEAGLE 01 WSO described it as 'solid white, smooth, with no edges', with no nacelles, pylons, or wings.<sup>25</sup> Fravor attempted a helmet lock using the [Joint Helmet Mounted Cuing System](/objects/joint-helmet-mounted-cuing-system) but was unsuccessful.<sup>26</sup> The AAV was observed moving north, south, east, and west while maintaining a consistent altitude, with Fravor noting that the displacements were minor.<sup>27</sup> Fravor stated the AAV appeared to recognise them, realigned its axis toward their aircraft, and at that moment the water disturbance ceased,<sup>28</sup> after which the AAV ascended quickly at supersonic speed; USS Princeton then reported it had appeared at the flight's [Combat Air Patrol (CAP)](/concepts/combat-air-patrol-cap) point.<sup>29</sup> Both Fravor and his WSO confirmed all aircraft systems were fully functional throughout the engagement, with no system malfunctions, no avionics issues, and no radio or communication interference.<sup>30</sup>

A second VFA-41 section, in which [Lieutenant Chad Underwood](/people/chad-underwood) served as WSO, launched following FASTEAGLE and was briefed on what the earlier flight had seen.<sup>31</sup> Underwood's F/A-18F carried an [ATFLIR Pod](/objects/atflir-pod) with the FLIR in Lock and Slaved mode and the APG-73 radar in Range While Search at 150-kilometre scale with 4-bar scan and interleave.<sup>32</sup> Initial radar tracks placed the AAV approximately 55 to 75 kilometres to the south of Underwood's aircraft.<sup>33</sup> Multiple attempts to transition the APG-73 to Single Target Track mode failed, with no jamming cues detected; Underwood stated it simply appeared as if the radar could not achieve a lock.<sup>34</sup> The AAV appeared stationary on the FLIR with no discernible movement other than closure resulting from the aircraft's own movement.<sup>35</sup> Underwood was explicit that he could not confirm the object tracked on FLIR was the same object described by Fravor's flight, as he never had a visual and only observed the object via FLIR.<sup>36</sup> The resulting footage was later released as the [Anomalous Aerial Vehicle (AAV) Forward Looking Infrared (FLIR) Recording 2004-11](/documents/anomalous-aerial-vehicle-aav-forward-looking-infrared-flir-recording-2004-11). After landing, Underwood refused to surrender his tapes to the [Carrier Intelligence Center (CVIC)](/organisations/carrier-intelligence-center-cvic), and copies were instead made with a set turned in to the intelligence section.<sup>37</sup>

The report's key assessments state that the AAV was no known aircraft or air vehicle in the inventory of the United States or any foreign nation,<sup>38</sup> that it exhibited advanced [low observability](/concepts/low-observability-stealth) characteristics at multiple radar bands rendering US radar-based engagement capabilities ineffective,<sup>39</sup> and that it possessed advanced aerodynamic performance with no visible control surfaces and no visible means to generate lift.<sup>40</sup> The AAV also exhibited advanced propulsion capability, remaining stationary then transitioning to velocities far greater than any known aerial vehicle with little to no visible signature.<sup>41</sup> The report notes the AAV possibly demonstrated the ability to cloak or become invisible to the human eye<sup>42</sup> and possibly the ability to operate undersea completely undetectable by US sensors,<sup>43</sup> though the latter assessment is qualified by USS Louisville's record: a qualified submarine officer and the former commander of [USS Louisville](/objects/uss-louisville) both confirmed there were no unidentified sonar contacts at any time during operations off the coast of California,<sup>44</sup> and based on that absence of detection the report assesses it as highly unlikely that an AAV operated below the ocean surface.<sup>45</sup> The AAV did not take any offensive action against the carrier strike group,<sup>46</sup> and the CSG-11 Director of Operations stated at no time did they consider the AAV a threat to the battle group.<sup>47</sup> Following the intercept, [David Fravor](/people/david-fravor) debriefed with the Carrier Air Wing Intelligence Officer at the [Carrier Intelligence Center (CVIC) Debrief](/events/carrier-intelligence-center-cvic-debrief);<sup>48</sup> the Carrier Air Wing Intelligence Officer completed an email-based [Carrier Strike Group 11 (CSG-11) Anomalous Aerial Vehicle (AAV) Mission Report (MISREP) 2004-11](/documents/carrier-strike-group-11-csg-11-anomalous-aerial-vehicle-aav-mission-report-misrep-2004-11) including the FLIR video and transmitted it via secure system to [3rd Fleet Intelligence](/organisations/3rd-fleet-intelligence),<sup>49</sup> where it was received and acknowledged but not forwarded further up the chain of command.<sup>50</sup> Rear Admiral [D.C. Curtis](/people/dc-curtis) commanded CSG-11 during the incident;<sup>51</sup> [Peter Daly](/people/peter-daly) assumed command of the strike group in 2005-01 with no knowledge of or involvement in the incident.<sup>52</sup>
