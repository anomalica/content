The [Nimitz Carrier Strike Group (CSG-11) AAV Incident Report](/documents/nimitz-carrier-strike-group-csg-11-aav-incident-report) is an internal [United States Navy](/organisations/united-states-navy) document dated 2004-11-14 that records the detection and airborne intercept of one or more [anomalous aerial vehicles (AAVs)](/topics/anomalous-aerial-vehicle-aav) by the [Carrier Strike Group 11 (CSG-11)](/organisations/carrier-strike-group-11-csg-11) during pre-deployment training off the coasts of California and Mexico. The report compiles radar data, aircrew testimony, and organisational details into an executive summary, a chronological account of each participant's involvement, and a set of key assessments. It constitutes the principal primary source for the [2004 USS Nimitz UAP encounter](/events/2004-uss-nimitz-uap-encounter).<sup>1</sup>

Between approximately 2004-11-10 and 2004-11-16, [CSG-11](/organisations/carrier-strike-group-11-csg-11) was completing Tailored Ships Training Availability (TSTA) in the Southern California Operating Area.<sup>1</sup> The strike group comprised the [USS Nimitz (CVN-68)](/objects/uss-nimitz-cvn-68), [USS Princeton (CG-59)](/objects/uss-princeton-cg-59), [USS Chafee (DDG-90)](/objects/uss-chafee-ddg-90), [USS Higgins (DDG-76)](/objects/uss-higgins-ddg-76), and the [USS Louisville (SSN-724)](/objects/uss-louisville-ssn-724).<sup>19</sup> The USS Nimitz was home to [Carrier Air Wing 11 (CVW-11)](/organisations/carrier-air-wing-11-cvw-11),<sup>20</sup> which included [Marine Fighter Attack Squadron 232 (VMFA-232)](/organisations/marine-fighter-attack-squadron-232-vmfa-232), [Strike Fighter Squadron 41 (VFA-41)](/organisations/strike-fighter-squadron-41-vfa-41), [Strike Fighter Squadron 14 (VFA-14)](/organisations/strike-fighter-squadron-14-vfa-14), [Strike Fighter Squadron 94 (VFA-94)](/organisations/strike-fighter-squadron-94-vfa-94), [Electronic Attack Squadron 135 (VAQ-135)](/organisations/electronic-attack-squadron-135-vaq-135), [Carrier Airborne Early Warning Squadron 117 (VAW-117)](/organisations/carrier-airborne-early-warning-squadron-117-vaw-117), [Helicopter Anti-Submarine Squadron 6 (HS-6)](/organisations/helicopter-anti-submarine-squadron-6-hs-6), and [Fleet Logistics Support Squadron 30 (VRC-30)](/organisations/fleet-logistics-support-squadron-30-vrc-30) Det 3.<sup>21</sup> Of these units, only [USS Princeton (CG-59)](/objects/uss-princeton-cg-59), [VAW-117](/organisations/carrier-airborne-early-warning-squadron-117-vaw-117), [VMFA-232](/organisations/marine-fighter-attack-squadron-232-vmfa-232), and [VFA-41](/organisations/strike-fighter-squadron-41-vfa-41) were directly involved in the detection and intercept of the AAV.<sup>22</sup>

The [USS Princeton (CG-59)](/objects/uss-princeton-cg-59), a Ticonderoga-class guided-missile cruiser and the first of its class to carry the upgraded [AN/SPY-1](/objects/anspy-1-radar) radar system,<sup>23</sup> detected AAVs on three separate occasions during the week.<sup>25</sup> The [AN/SPY-1](/objects/anspy-1-radar) is rated at 4 megawatts and is capable of tracking over 100 targets simultaneously,<sup>24</sup> yet because it was configured for air intercept of conventional aircraft rather than ballistic missile tracking, it failed to obtain an accurate track of the contacts and dropped them as false targets.<sup>26</sup> The objects would descend from above approximately 18,000 metres to approximately 15 metres in a matter of seconds,<sup>3</sup> hover briefly, then depart at high velocities and turn rates.<sup>4</sup> A senior chief aboard USS Princeton, drawing on 17 years of experience on Aegis cruisers, assessed that the AAV exhibited ballistic missile characteristics in appearance, velocity, and radar indications.<sup>27</sup> The [E-2 Hawkeye](/objects/e-2-hawkeye) airborne from [VAW-117](/organisations/carrier-airborne-early-warning-squadron-117-vaw-117), equipped with the [AN/APS-145 radar](/objects/anaps-145-radar) from [Lockheed Martin](/organisations/lockheed-martin), detected the contact only after the [USS Princeton](/objects/uss-princeton-cg-59) directed the [E-2C](/objects/e-2-hawkeye) to the general direction; without that cue the return would likely have been missed.<sup>28</sup> On 2004-11-14, USS Princeton handed control of a flight of [F/A-18](/objects/fa-18)s to conduct an airborne intercept, vectoring them to within approximately 1.6 kilometres of the object.<sup>6</sup>

[David Fravor](/people/david-fravor), Commanding Officer of [VFA-41](/organisations/strike-fighter-squadron-41-vfa-41) and pilot of FastEagle 01,<sup>29</sup> and [Alex Dietrich](/people/alex-dietrich), pilot of FastEagle 02,<sup>30</sup> were the lead crew of the first [F/A-18](/objects/fa-18)F section to intercept the AAV. Visual contact was obtained at approximately N31 20' W117 10', roughly 130 kilometres south of the US/Mexico border and 56 kilometres off the Baja Mexico coast.<sup>31</sup> Fravor's first indication of the contact was a localised water disturbance below the object, which he described as frothy waves and foam almost as if the water was boiling.<sup>32</sup> The AAV itself was described as an elongated egg or "Tic Tac" shape with a discernible midline horizontal axis,<sup>33</sup> uniformly white, approximately 14 metres in length,<sup>33</sup> with no nacelles, pylons, or wings, and neither glowing nor reflecting sunlight.<sup>34</sup> Fravor's aircraft carried a [Joint Helmet Mounted Cueing System](/objects/joint-helmet-mounted-cueing-system) and an [AN/APG-73 radar](/objects/anapg-73-radar), but the radar showed no targets and a helmet lock attempt was unsuccessful.<sup>35</sup> As Fravor manoeuvred to close on the object, the AAV appeared to realign its axis toward the aircraft and the water disturbance ceased,<sup>36</sup> after which it ascended at supersonic speed.<sup>37</sup> The [USS Princeton](/objects/uss-princeton-cg-59) subsequently reported the AAV had repositioned to the training combat air patrol point and climbed to approximately 7,300 metres.<sup>38</sup> The [FastEagle](/organisations/strike-fighter-squadron-41-vfa-41) flight found no trace of either the AAV or the water disturbance when it attempted to re-acquire.<sup>39</sup> A second [F/A-18](/objects/fa-18)F section flown by [Chad Underwood](/people/chad-underwood) of [VFA-41](/organisations/strike-fighter-squadron-41-vfa-41)<sup>40</sup> subsequently detected the object via forward-looking infrared (FLIR) at an estimated altitude of 4,600 to 6,100 metres.<sup>41</sup> Underwood attempted multiple radar lock transitions to Single Target Track mode, but the radar could not lock; he observed no jamming cues, describing it as though the radar could not receive sufficient information to form a track file.<sup>42</sup> The FLIR showed the object as stationary until it moved out of the field of view.<sup>43</sup>

The report's key assessments concluded that the AAV matched no known aircraft or air vehicle in the inventory of the [United States](/organisations/united-states-navy) or any foreign nation;<sup>12</sup> that it exhibited advanced low observable characteristics across multiple radar bands, rendering US radar-based engagement capabilities ineffective;<sup>13</sup> and that it demonstrated aerodynamic performance with no visible control surfaces and no visible means to generate lift.<sup>14</sup> The report also assessed that the AAV remained stationary with little variation in altitude and transitioned to velocities far exceeding those of any known aerial vehicle with little to no visible signature,<sup>15</sup> and noted the possibility that it could cloak or become invisible to the human eye.<sup>16</sup> Regarding possible subsurface activity, the [USS Louisville (SSN-724)](/objects/uss-louisville-ssn-724) reported no unidentified sonar contacts in the vicinity of the sightings,<sup>44</sup> a finding confirmed by its former commander,<sup>45</sup> leading the report to assess that subsurface operation by the AAV was highly unlikely, while leaving open the possibility of a cloaking capability consistent with pilot observations of the water disturbance.<sup>17</sup>

In terms of reporting and administrative follow-up, the Carrier Air Wing Intelligence Officer completed an email-based Mission Report (MISREP) and transmitted it with the FLIR video to [United States Third Fleet](/organisations/united-states-third-fleet) Intelligence.<sup>46</sup> The MISREP was received by the 3rd Fleet intelligence staff but was not forwarded up the chain of command, was not archived, and was likely deleted, as no requirement existed to retain training reports of that kind.<sup>47</sup> All AAV activity had been reported to Rear Admiral [D. C. Curtis](/people/d-c-curtis) by the Commanding Officers of [USS Princeton](/objects/uss-princeton-cg-59) and [VFA-41](/organisations/strike-fighter-squadron-41-vfa-41).<sup>48</sup> The [CSG-11](/organisations/carrier-strike-group-11-csg-11) Director of Operations confirmed that, beyond the MISREP, no other official report was produced and the AAV was at no time considered a threat to the battle group. [CSG-11](/organisations/carrier-strike-group-11-csg-11) personnel stated they had no prior knowledge of any live fire events, weapons testing, or experimental aircraft operating in the area, and had never encountered anything resembling the AAV before or since.<sup>49</sup>
