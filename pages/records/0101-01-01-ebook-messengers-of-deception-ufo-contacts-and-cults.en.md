According to Messengers of Deception: UFO Contacts and Cults, the book examines the social and political consequences of belief in an outer-space invasion, whether real or simulated.<sup>1</sup> [Jacques Vallée](/people/jacques-vallee) describes research into occult groups and contactee movements, including participation in some Californian groups as a “participant-observer”.<sup>2</sup> He reports finding no single, consistent belief system among the groups, while finding that each claimed some form of contact with an alien intelligence.<sup>3</sup> Vallée distinguishes “direct contactees”, who claim to have witnessed an unexplained physical phenomenon and developed a personal relationship with its supposed intelligence, from “indirect contactees”, who have not witnessed such a phenomenon but believe they possess a personal link to its cause.<sup>4</sup><sup>5</sup>

The source uses case studies to connect [Unidentified Flying Object (UFO)](/topics/unidentified-flying-object-ufo) reports with religious, psychological and political activity. Vallée concludes that UFOs may be physical devices affecting human consciousness and social change.<sup>6</sup> He discusses Human Individual Metamorphosis (H.I.M.), later known as Heaven's Gate, whose followers were told to abandon earthly possessions; according to the source, its members committed collective suicide in March 1997.<sup>7</sup><sup>8</sup> The book also describes the 1994 killings associated with the Order of the Solar Temple in Canada, France and Switzerland, which Vallée considered an example of dangerous mind control and simulated extraterrestrial contact.<sup>9</sup><sup>10</sup> Its discussion of the [cattle mutilations phenomenon](/topics/cattle-mutilations-phenomenon) notes reported associations with UFO sightings and records 700 reports across 15 western US states during an 18-month period before January 1977.<sup>11</sup><sup>12</sup> The book also reviews hypnosis research suggesting that many aspects of abduction experiences may originate in witnesses’ minds.<sup>13</sup> Vallée ultimately presents the UFO phenomenon as physical, psycho-physiological and social, while finding no evidence that it represents visitors from outer space.<sup>14</sup><sup>15</sup> Its significance lies in treating contact claims not only as questions of physical explanation, but also as subjects involving belief, persuasion and social change; Vallée warned that faith in extraterrestrial salvation could expose believers to manipulation.<sup>16</sup> He further considered the expectation of space visitors a source of new religious, cultural and political concepts.<sup>17</sup>
