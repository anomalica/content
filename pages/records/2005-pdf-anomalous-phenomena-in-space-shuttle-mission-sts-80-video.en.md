In this document, Mark J. Carlotto analyses video from the STS-80 mission, recorded by a camera aboard the [Space Shuttle Columbia](/objects/space-shuttle-columbia). He presents three categories of phenomena: two slow-moving circular objects, fast-moving objects near the shuttle, and a luminous apparition near the Earth’s surface.<sup>1</sup> The mission began on 1996-11-19 and lasted 17 days, with objectives including the deployment and retrieval of two satellites and two space walks.<sup>2</sup> On 1996-12-02, the payload-bay camera recorded disk-like objects near the shuttle at approximately 07:57 GMT.<sup>3</sup> Carlotto identifies the first luminous event as STS-80 video event F1 (1996-12-02): it crosses the camera’s field of view and is followed by a zoom-out.<sup>4</sup> He estimates its speed at over approximately 800 kilometres per second,<sup>5</sup> and places it near the eastern coast of Puerto Rico USA, close to Vieques, using a correction for foreshortening (remote sensing geometry).<sup>6</sup> The event is discussed as occurring before sunrise and away from thunderstorm activity.<sup>7</sup>

The analysis also selects ten streaks, S1 to S10, for study.<sup>8</sup> Carlotto says that their speeds and directions imply that they were not shuttle debris or meteors,<sup>9</sup> while considering F1’s shape and motion inconsistent with typical orbiting debris.<sup>10</sup> His proposed explanations remain differentiated: he considers the STS-80 video disk-like object sightings (1996-12-02) likely to involve nearby ice particles or other small objects,<sup>11</sup> whereas he suggests that F1 might be a new atmospheric phenomenon observed from space.<sup>12</sup> The source records a contrasting account attributed to James Oberg, who dismissed the disk-like-object event as identical in origin to scenes from STS-48 and other shuttle flights.<sup>13</sup> Oberg’s explanation involved particles becoming visible when the orbiter entered sunlight.<sup>14</sup> The account also reports that crewman Story Musgrave saw nothing unusual at the relevant point in the flight.<sup>15</sup> The document therefore matters as a detailed record of competing interpretations, image geometry and motion analysis; it also notes that F1 was not mentioned in the mission record.<sup>16</sup>
