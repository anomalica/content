The source examines the Merivalja object, described as a buried structure beneath a garden in Estonia, Tallinn, Merivalja. Ross Coulthart said that Virgo Mitt struck metal while digging a well and that fragments were later tested.<sup>1</sup> [Mart Sander](/people/mart-sander) said that the object was first dug up in 1969.<sup>2</sup> Ross Coulthart said that Soviet scientists, researchers, [Committee for State Security (KGB)](/organisations/committee-for-state-security-kgb) agents and military personnel investigated it for around ten years.<sup>3</sup> Soviet investigators reportedly described it as an oval metal object about 15 metres long and weighing 200 tonnes, partly beneath the house and extending into the garden.<sup>4</sup> Ross Coulthart also said that an attempted excavation in the 1980s was halted after a worker reportedly received a blow from the object.<sup>5</sup>

The account presents the material evidence as incomplete and disputed. Sander said that only fragments of the supposed outer layer had been recovered,<sup>6</sup> while later tests, conducted between 2012 and 2017, produced results different from those of the 1980s and left researchers uncertain whether the original pieces had been tested.<sup>7</sup> Coulthart said that the KGB became involved and marked its files “sovacheno sekretno”, meaning completely secret.<sup>8</sup> The source also profiles [Alexander Deyev](/people/alexander-deyev), whom Coulthart described as an aviation engineer with ties to the Soviet Defense Ministry.<sup>9</sup> Coulthart said that Deyev established a laboratory above the object and allegedly used D-generators, including a smoky glass orb (D field generator), in an attempt to bring it to the surface.<sup>10</sup> Sander described Deyev as claiming telepathic contact with non-human objects.<sup>11</sup>

The source emphasises the limits of the available record. Coulthart said that no photograph of the actual object had been produced and that the Soviet excavation team never reached it.<sup>12</sup> The 1991 Japanese investigation of the Merivalja garden reportedly used ground-penetrating radar and electromagnetic sensing,<sup>13</sup> but Coulthart said there is no published record of its findings.<sup>14</sup> Sander said that research groups have proposed natural, man-made and intermediate explanations for the object.<sup>15</sup> The source therefore matters as a record of competing claims, investigative activity and evidential uncertainty surrounding a prominent Estonian UAP-associated mystery.
