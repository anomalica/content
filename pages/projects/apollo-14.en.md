Apollo 14 was NASA's third crewed Moon landing, commanded by [Alan Shepard](/people/alan-shepard), who underwent a secret and then-innovative inner-ear surgical procedure that allowed him to command the mission.<sup>1</sup> [Edgar Mitchell](/people/edgar-mitchell) piloted the lunar module Antares (Apollo 14 Lunar Module) to Fra Mauro (Apollo 14 landing site) during the 1971 Apollo 14 landing on 1971-02-05, becoming the sixth man to walk on the Moon.<sup>2</sup> Only Shepard and Mitchell descended to the lunar surface, and each carried a single Hasselblad camera.<sup>3</sup>

During the mission's final extra-vehicular activity, Shepard surprised [National Aeronautics and Space Administration (NASA)](/organisations/national-aeronautics-and-space-administration-nasa) and the nation on live television by taking the first golf shot on the Moon.<sup>4</sup> Two days earlier, Mitchell had erected a colour television camera at the landing site that broadcast a running sequence of images to Earth in parallel with Shepard's live radio transmission.<sup>5</sup> Shepard boasted during that transmission that the ball "went miles and miles."<sup>6</sup> A photograph from the same excursion, AS14-66-9301, known as "Mitchell Under Glass," shows Shepard's image of Mitchell setting up the television camera.<sup>7</sup>

A separate strand of material concerns anomalous lights Mitchell reported seeing during the mission. The Spaceman, a close friend of Mitchell's who wishes to remain anonymous,<sup>8</sup> said that Mitchell privately confided seeing anomalous objects during the mission that he could not explain.<sup>9</sup> According to The Spaceman, Mitchell described well-defined blue lights that appeared to have a structure behind them.<sup>10</sup> A photograph taken outside the lunar module on the Moon's surface shows Mitchell posing in the foreground with a blue light hovering in the distant blackness of space behind him.<sup>11</sup> The Spaceman said that Mitchell dismissed the possibility that the lights were lens flares, reflections from inside the lunar module, or camera glitches, because he had seen them with his own eyes and later confirmed they appeared on film.<sup>12</sup> The Spaceman also said that Mitchell, anticipating critics would call the light in the photograph a lens artefact or flare, maintained that he had seen it directly and did not accept that explanation.<sup>13</sup>

Mitchell reported seeing an object with a blue light from the lunar module just before docking with the command module.<sup>14</sup> High-resolution lunar module film of the return journey, taken as the lunar module waited for the command module to rotate for docking, shows a trio of blue lights grouped in what looks like a dark triangular shape to the left of the command module against the blackness of space.<sup>15</sup> According to The Spaceman, Mitchell said the same blue-light anomaly appeared on two separate films from two separate cameras, anticipating it would be dismissed as a lens flare or reflection.<sup>16</sup> The camera that photographed Mitchell on the lunar surface with the light behind him remained on the Moon,<sup>17</sup> while the camera that filmed the lights from the lunar module toward the command module returned to Earth with Mitchell.<sup>18</sup> There were no blue lights inside the lunar module that could have caused a reflection, as all its interior lights were white.<sup>19</sup> According to The Spaceman, Mitchell always publicly denied that he saw a UFO during the mission, but confided that he saw strange objects throughout his 1971 lunar mission.<sup>20</sup>

Separately, Ken Johnston, who held the Apollo photographic archive, recounted that after the mission film was processed at the NASA photo lab he organised a private viewing for chief astronomer Thornton Page and his associates.<sup>21</sup> Johnston recalled setting the film up in a sequence projector, similar to a reversed gun camera, that allowed stopping, freeze-framing, forwarding, reversing and zooming.<sup>22</sup> He recounted that footage of the Moon's far side showed a cluster of about five or six lights inside the shadowed rim of a large crater,<sup>23</sup> where the sun angle meant the crater's shadowed interior covered about half of it.<sup>24</sup> Most of the prints in Johnston's collection were from the [Apollo 12](/projects/apollo-12) and Apollo 14 missions.<sup>25</sup>

Richard C. Hoagland later examined Johnston's Apollo 14 prints and found that computer processing could reveal geometric detail in the pitch-black areas of the lunar sky that was not visible to the naked eye.<sup>26</sup> Among Johnston's photographs, Hoagland discovered two untouched panoramas of the Apollo 14 landing site, each a mosaic of individual Hasselblad images printed on two 8x10 glossies.<sup>27</sup> The Apollo 14 and Apollo 12 landing sites, in Oceanus Procellarum, were the closest together of any Apollo missions, separated by approximately 196 kilometres.<sup>28</sup> Hoagland said he became more convinced his ancient lunar dome hypothesis was correct because Apollo 14 and Apollo 12 had photographed what he interpreted as the same crystalline geometry in generally the same location,<sup>29</sup> the two sites lying approximately 196 kilometres apart.<sup>30</sup> During a 1996 debate with Mitchell on Coast to Coast AM, Hoagland said the distant lunar ruins he identified might have been very faint and further filtered by the gold visors worn by the astronauts on the lunar surface.<sup>31</sup>
