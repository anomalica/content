Project Blue Book was a [United States Air Force](/organisations/united-states-air-force) programme established in 1952<sup>1</sup> by Director of Intelligence Major General [Charles P. Cabell](/people/charles-p-cabell)<sup>2</sup> to investigate reports of [unidentified flying objects (UFOs)](/topics/unidentified-flying-object-ufo). Based at Wright-Patterson Air Force Base near Dayton, Ohio, it became the longest-running official UFO investigation in US history.<sup>3</sup> It was the third such programme, following [Project Sign](/projects/project-sign) and [Project GRUDGE](/projects/project-grudge).<sup>4</sup> [J. Allen Hynek](/people/j-allen-hynek) served as the programme's lead scientific investigator,<sup>5</sup> while the directorship rotated through several officers — Captain [Edward Ruppelt](/people/edward-ruppelt), Captain Charles Hardin, Captain George T. Gregory, Lieutenant Colonel Roger J. Friend, and Lieutenant Colonel Hector Quintanilla Jr.<sup>6</sup> — with Ruppelt serving as its first chief.<sup>7</sup> The [Battelle Memorial Institute (BMI)](/organisations/battelle-memorial-institute-bmi) provided a punch-card system to automate the statistical analysis of case holdings.<sup>8</sup> In 1953, the [US Air Force](/organisations/us-air-force) created the [4602d Air Intelligence Service Squadron](/organisations/4602d-air-intelligence-service-squadron) to vet all UAP reports confidentially before they were forwarded to Project Blue Book, culling cases that raised national security concerns.<sup>9</sup>

Over its operational life the programme received and catalogued 12,618 reports from both civilian and military witnesses.<sup>10</sup> Cases were sorted into one of three categories: identified, insufficient data, and unidentified.<sup>11</sup> Of the total intake, 701 reports were classified as unidentified and were never resolved.<sup>12</sup> Common sources of misidentification included planets such as Venus, Jupiter, and Mars when observed through haze or moving cloud,<sup>13</sup> and balloon-related phenomena — weather balloons, radiosondes, and large research balloons — which together accounted for several thousand cases.<sup>14</sup> The programme's formal findings, as later cited by the [All-domain Anomaly Resolution Office (AARO)](/organisations/all-domain-anomaly-resolution-office-aaro), were that no UFO reported under Project Blue Book demonstrated any indication of a threat to national security,<sup>15</sup> that there was no evidence of technological developments or principles beyond the range of then-contemporary scientific knowledge,<sup>16</sup> and that no sightings categorised as unidentified provided evidence of extraterrestrial vehicles.<sup>17</sup>

Both Ruppelt and Hynek later described internal pressure that compromised the programme's integrity. Ruppelt wrote that he was expected to explain away every report and that the [US Air Force](/organisations/us-air-force) sought press coverage aligned with its institutional position.<sup>18</sup> Hynek stated that the Air Force expected him to perform the role of debunker.<sup>19</sup> [Harry Turner](/people/harry-turner), writing in 1971, asserted that as early as 1953 the [Air Force Office of Special Investigations (AFOSI)](/organisations/air-force-office-of-special-investigations-afosi) had persuaded the Air Force to use Project Blue Book as a vehicle for public debunking while a separate, secret programme pursued anti-gravity research.<sup>20</sup> Hynek himself later came to regret the role he had played in suppressing UAP evidence under the Air Force's aegis.<sup>21</sup> A notable episode occurred in March 1966 when sightings disturbed neighbourhoods in Michigan over several days; at the insistence of Michigan congressman [Gerald Ford](/people/gerald-ford), Hynek's team reviewed the cases and announced that the witnesses had seen swamp gas.<sup>22</sup> The [Ford Presidential Library](/people/gerald-ford) subsequently released at least fifteen documents related to that finding, which Ford remained sceptical of throughout his life.<sup>23</sup> The [Condon Report](/documents/condon-report), an independent university study commissioned in the mid-1960s, was criticised by some scientists including Hynek, but it provided the Air Force the grounds it sought to close the programme.<sup>24</sup>

Secretary of the Air Force [Robert C. Seamans Jr.](/people/robert-c-jr-seamans) announced Project Blue Book's termination on 1969-12-17,<sup>25</sup> stating that the programme could no longer be justified either on grounds of national security or in the interest of science.<sup>26</sup> After closure, the [CIA](/organisations/cia) and broader government effectively ignored [unidentified aerial phenomena (UAP)](/topics/unidentified-aerial-phenomena-uap) for decades. [Daniel Sheehan](/people/daniel-sheehan), a Jesuit lawyer, recounts that on a Saturday morning in late spring 1977 he attended the Madison wing of the Library of Congress to view classified Project Blue Book microfiche at the invitation of officials seeking to brief President [Jimmy Carter](/people/jimmy-carter).<sup>27</sup> Sheehan states that among those materials he found photographs of a crashed, dome-topped craft that had ploughed a trench across a snow-covered field, with [US Air Force](/organisations/us-air-force) personnel visible around it,<sup>28</sup> and that one photograph showed symbols etched into the side of the craft below the dome, which he traced onto the inside cardboard backing of a legal notepad he had brought into the room.<sup>29</sup> This [event](/events/daniel-sheehan-blue-book-viewing) remains contested and unverified through any independently corroborated source. A report prepared by the [Australian Department of Defence](/organisations/australian-department-of-defence) in the 1970s, cited by [Luis Elizondo](/people/luis-elizondo), characterised Project Blue Book's strategy as erecting a "façade of ridicule" intended to allay public alarm, reduce Soviet exploitation of UAP mass sightings, and provide cover for a US programme developing vehicles that emulate UAP performance.<sup>30</sup>

Following termination, the [All-domain Anomaly Resolution Office (AARO)](/organisations/all-domain-anomaly-resolution-office-aaro) partnered with the United States National Archives to examine Project Blue Book's records, which spanned from 1947 to 1969<sup>31</sup> and amounted to 7,252 files containing a total of 65,778 digital records.<sup>32</sup> AARO's review noted a spike in reported sightings between 1952 and 1957 and another in 1960. The programme's closure left approximately a 40-year gap in official US government UAP investigation before the establishment of successor programmes.<sup>33</sup> The [Advanced Aerospace Weapon Systems Applications Program (AAWSAP)](/projects/advanced-aerospace-weapon-systems-applications-program-advanced-aerospace-weapon-system-applications-program-advanced-aerospace-weapon-systems-applications-program-aawsap) and the [Advanced Aerospace Threat Identification Program](/organisations/advanced-aerospace-threat-identification-program) later reviewed both new cases and older Project Blue Book cases as part of their UAP research activity.<sup>34</sup> At a congressional UAP hearing in May 2022, a senior Pentagon intelligence official, when asked about research into other UAP programmes, named only the Advanced Aerospace Threat Identification Program and Blue Book.<sup>35</sup> [David Fravor](/people/david-fravor) has stated his view that a disinformation campaign to deny the existence of [Unidentified Anomalous Phenomena](/concepts/unidentified-anomalous-phenomena) operated during Project Blue Book's years, though he noted he does not speak for the current US government on that point.
