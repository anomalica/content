The term "unidentified flying object" (UFO) entered popular usage after pilot [Kenneth Arnold](/people/kenneth-arnold) reported seeing a formation of unusual aircraft near Mount Rainier in 1947, with media coverage of his account giving rise to the phrase "flying saucer."<sup>1</sup> Public interest in the subject expanded rapidly from that point, evidenced by the proliferation of television programmes, books, films, and later podcasts devoted to the topic.<sup>2</sup> The [United States Air Force](/organisations/united-states-air-force) responded to the wave of reports with a succession of formal investigation programmes. [Project Sign](/projects/project-sign) was established to determine whether the objects might be Soviet secret weapons or extra-planetary in origin<sup>3</sup> and evaluated 243 reported sightings.<sup>4</sup> Project Sign's staff allegedly drafted a document called the [Estimate of the Situation (1948)](/documents/estimate-of-the-situation-1948), which reportedly concluded that at least some UFOs were interplanetary;<sup>5</sup> Department of Defense (DoD) leadership rejected the report for lack of proof, and some accounts hold that this rejection contributed to the establishment of [Project GRUDGE](/projects/project-grudge), which certain sources characterise as institutionally inclined to debunk UFO claims.<sup>6</sup> Project GRUDGE investigated 244 reports<sup>7</sup> and at least one account asserts its goals were explicitly to discount and explain away all such reports.<sup>8</sup>

[Project Blue Book](/projects/project-blue-book) commenced in 1952<sup>9</sup> and became the longest-running of the official American UFO investigation programmes. Over the full span of the [United States Air Force](/organisations/united-states-air-force)'s studies from 1947 to 1969, the service recorded 12,618 UFO sightings.<sup>10</sup> Its analysis concluded that the majority involved misidentified stars, clouds, conventional aircraft, or spy planes, though 701 reports remained unexplained.<sup>11</sup> No case investigated under Project Blue Book demonstrated any indication of a threat to national security.<sup>12</sup> The [Battelle Memorial Institute (BMI)](/organisations/battelle-memorial-institute-bmi) contributed technical support to the programme, developing a punch-card system for statistical analysis of the accumulated reports.<sup>13</sup> A surge in sightings in 1952, particularly those drawing attention over [D.C. Washington](/places/district-of-columbia-washington-usa) during that summer - later recorded as the [1952 Washington, D.C. UFO sightings](/events/dc-unidentified-flying-object-ufo-sightings-1952-washington) - prompted the [CIA](/organisations/central-intelligence-agency-cia) to task its [Office of Scientific Intelligence (OSI)](/organisations/cia-office-of-scientific-intelligence-osi) with reviewing UFO cases.<sup>14</sup> The resulting [CIA Special Study Group 1952](/organisations/cia-special-study-group-1952) assessed that 90 per cent of reports were explainable, while rejecting the possibility that the remainder represented Soviet or extraterrestrial technology.<sup>15</sup> [The Robertson Panel](/organisations/the-robertson-panel), convened shortly thereafter, unanimously concluded there was no evidence UFOs posed a direct threat to national security or were of extraterrestrial origin,<sup>16</sup> but recommended that the government use various channels to debunk UFO reports and proposed monitoring domestic UFO enthusiast organisations.<sup>17</sup> A classified University of Colorado study commissioned in the mid-1960s produced the [Condon Report](/documents/condon-report), whose general conclusion held that nothing from two decades of UFO study had advanced scientific knowledge.<sup>18</sup>

A significant structural factor explaining the volume of UFO reports was the concurrent surge in classified American aviation and technology programmes. According to a declassified CIA assessment, more than half of the UFO reports investigated in the 1950s and 1960s were assessed to have been US reconnaissance flights.<sup>19</sup> More broadly, investigators concluded that some portion of unidentified aerial phenomena (UAP) misidentifications almost certainly resulted from observers unknowingly witnessing new technological developments.<sup>20</sup> Alongside the misidentification problem, [United States Air Force](/organisations/united-states-air-force) officials and senator [Harry Reid](/people/harry-reid) later noted that a cultural stigma within military institutions meant UFO sightings were often not reported up the chain of command, as service members feared ridicule.<sup>21</sup>

Interest in the subject was renewed in the 2010s when the [Advanced Aerospace Threat Identification Program (AATIP)](/organisations/advanced-aerospace-threat-identification-program) — a programme run out of the Pentagon and tasked with investigating UFO reports<sup>22</sup> — was publicly disclosed. AATIP collected video and audio recordings of reported incidents, including footage from a [United States Navy](/organisations/united-states-navy) F/A-18 Super Hornet showing an object surrounded by a glowing aura travelling at high speed.<sup>23</sup> Among the cases linked to AATIP was the [2004 USS Nimitz UFO incident](/events/2004-uss-nimitz-unidentified-flying-object-ufo-incident), in which naval aviator [David Fravor](/people/david-fravor) reported observing and pursuing an unidentified object;<sup>24</sup> Fravor later described the object as white and tic-tac-shaped, with his crew positioned approximately 6,000 metres above it when first sighted.<sup>25</sup> Senator [Harry Reid](/people/harry-reid), whose interest in UFOs had been shaped in part by aerospace entrepreneur [Robert Bigelow](/people/robert-bigelow),<sup>9</sup> was among those who had supported the programme's funding.

The most recent phase of official US engagement with the subject followed disclosures by intelligence community officer [David Grusch](/people/david-grusch), who provided classified evidence to Congress and two inspectors general alleging that UFO-related information had been improperly withheld from congressional oversight.<sup>26</sup> The Office of the Intelligence Community Inspector General assessed the allegation to be "urgent and credible."<sup>27</sup> The [United States Department of Defense (DoD)](/organisations/united-states-department-of-defense-dod) responded with a statement that its new UFO office had "not discovered any verifiable information to substantiate claims that any programs regarding the possession or reverse-engineering of extraterrestrial materials have existed in the past or exist currently."<sup>28</sup> The [All-domain Anomaly Resolution Office (AARO)](/organisations/all-domain-anomaly-resolution-office-aaro) now serves as the Defense Department's designated body for UFO analysis.<sup>29</sup> The broader [Unidentified Anomalous Phenomena](/concepts/unidentified-anomalous-phenomena) designation — the term that has largely replaced UFO in official US government usage — reflects an expanded scope of inquiry. A persistent obstacle across all investigative eras has been what observers and researchers identify as institutional stigma: the subject's association with fringe culture has discouraged both witnesses from coming forward and scientists from engaging with the evidence on a formal basis.<sup>30</sup>
