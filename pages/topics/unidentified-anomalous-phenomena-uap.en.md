Unidentified Anomalous Phenomena (UAP) is the official term for objects once called [Unidentified Flying Object (UFO)](/topics/unidentified-flying-object-ufo). The UAP Task Force was established to investigate such objects under the newer designation.<sup>1</sup> In official reporting, unidentified does not by itself establish an extraordinary origin. The [United States Department of Defense (DoD)](/organisations/united-states-department-of-defense-dod) stated in 2020 that the aerial phenomena shown in three released [United States Navy (USN)](/organisations/united-states-navy-usn) videos remained unidentified.<sup>2</sup> [Luis Elizondo](/people/luis-elizondo) said that the [Advanced Aerospace Threat Identification Program (AATIP)](/projects/advanced-aerospace-threat-identification-program-aatip) had found a real phenomenon in the sky, while its nature, method of operation, intent and full capabilities remained unknown.<sup>3</sup> Recorded cases vary in their reported characteristics: one report described two round, white and thermally hot UAP moving south at approximately 440 kilometres per hour,<sup>4</sup> while an aircrew observed one UAP in the Arabian Gulf on 2020-08-08 without impact to its mission.<sup>5</sup> A separate sighting near Deir ez-Zor on 2022-07-31 lasted less than a minute and involved movement from north to south.<sup>6</sup>

Government investigation has included dedicated programmes and the routine reporting systems of the armed services. The existence and investigative role of AATIP were described by Christopher Sherwood, and the programme was tasked with investigating UFOs and UAP.<sup>7</sup> Luis Elizondo took over AATIP in 2010 and focused on national-security implications of UAP documented by US service members.<sup>8</sup> The [United States Air Force (USAF)](/organisations/united-states-air-force-usaf) and USN have been at the forefront of developing policies, procedures and mechanisms for reporting UAP, while the DoD has relied heavily on its Services and Components to detect, report, collect, analyse and identify them since the 1940s.<sup>9</sup> The U.S. Department of Defense Inspector General nevertheless determined that the DoD had not used a coordinated approach for those activities.<sup>10</sup> The same source reported that the DoD had not issued a comprehensive response plan identifying roles, responsibilities, requirements and coordination procedures.<sup>11</sup> In its historical review, the [All-domain Anomaly Resolution Office (AARO)](/organisations/all-domain-anomaly-resolution-office-aaro) and its predecessor organisations concluded that most reported cases concerned ordinary objects, atmospheric or natural phenomena, or observer misidentification.<sup>12</sup>

The subject has also become associated with questions of classification, oversight and disclosure. According to the unclassified complaint of [David Charles Grusch](/people/david-charles-grusch), UAP-related classified information was withheld or concealed from the [United States Congress](/organisations/united-states-congress) by elements of the intelligence community in order to thwart legitimate congressional oversight.<sup>13</sup> From late 2021 to July 2022, Grusch was the National Geospatial-Intelligence Agency's co-lead for UAP analysis and its representative to the UAP Task Force.<sup>14</sup> [Christopher K. Mellon](/people/christopher-k-mellon) arranged classified briefings for members of Congress and other officials that included references to exotic retrieved materials.<sup>15</sup> The US Senate's Intelligence Committee ordered the Director of National Intelligence and the Secretary of Defense to deliver a report on UAP sightings.<sup>16</sup> Elizondo stated that, over the previous decade and a half, certain UAP programmes had operated without a watchful Congress, a responsive Executive Branch or an informed public.<sup>17</sup> A report discussed in 2024 included 366 sightings, of which 171 remained uncharacterised.<sup>18</sup>

UAP reports are assessed through observations, sensor data and competing explanations. [Ryan Graves](/people/ryan-graves) said that his F/A-18 squadron began seeing UAP hovering over restricted airspace southeast of Virginia Beach in 2014.<sup>19</sup> He considered simultaneous radar and infrared detection difficult to spoof.<sup>20</sup> Explanations proposed in the sources include advanced human technology, foreign technology, natural phenomena, perceptual error and non-human origin. Mellon considered Russian or Chinese technology possible, while also saying that the reported performance appeared extraordinary; he further considered it necessary to entertain an alien-origin possibility for objects that appeared not to be American, Russian or Chinese.<sup>21</sup><sup>22</sup> [Phil McGraw](/people/phil-mcgraw) reported that some UAP had appeared on radar to move at approximately 64,000 kilometres per hour and execute a 90-degree turn.<sup>23</sup> According to the USPER narrative, ground teams in a late-2025 encounter detected a UAP on forward-looking infrared (FLIR), describing it as super-hot, low to the ground and moving east then south at high speed.<sup>24</sup> The account further said that the object split into two and changed direction.<sup>25</sup> These reports describe observations or testimony; they do not, on their own, establish the cause of the observations.

Research methods and the scope of available evidence remain central to the topic. [Avi Loeb](/people/avi-loeb) recommends examining fresh UAP data collected by the US government and military using improved sensors.<sup>26</sup> [National Aeronautics and Space Administration (NASA)](/organisations/national-aeronautics-and-space-administration-nasa) has also been part of the institutional discussion: [Ross Coulthart](/people/ross-coulthart) said that the terms of reference for NASA's UAP study team limited it to aerial or terrestrial UAP,<sup>27</sup> and reported that NASA administrator Bill Nelson stated there was no evidence of anomalous UAP in NASA imagery.<sup>28</sup> Loeb characterised the objects under investigation as either human-made or non-human-made.<sup>29</sup> Other interpretations extend beyond physical technology. Julia Mossbridge argued that UAP research must recognise a relationship between the human mind, mind as a universal concept and the phenomenon.<sup>30</sup> [Whitley Strieber](/people/whitley-strieber) said that knowledge within the US defence establishment was concentrated on materials and biology rather than aims, motives or relationships with the soul.<sup>31</sup> Coulthart also reported a claim that a Pentagon briefing described the phenomenon as capable of manipulating human perception and remotely interfering with what people see and experience; that claim is presented as reported testimony rather than an established finding.<sup>32</sup>
