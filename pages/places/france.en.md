France appears repeatedly in the source record as a site of independent UFO research activity, running in parallel to, and at times sceptical of, official American conclusions on the subject. Researcher [Jacques Vallée](/people/jacques-vallee), who emigrated from France in 1962, brought UFO sighting data from Europe that augmented the [Project Blue Book](/projects/project-blue-book) files held at Northwestern University<sup>1</sup>, and later returned with [Janine Vallée](/people/janine-vallee) to investigate the Carteret sighting during a research trip through western France in December 1973<sup>2</sup>. Vallée's computer analysis of French cases in 1964 was the first to establish that UFO landings tended to occur in desolate places<sup>3</sup>.

French institutional response to the subject was mixed. A 1966 rumour that the US Air Force was seeking a negative report was taken seriously enough in Paris to prevent the creation of a French investigation committee modelled on the American one<sup>4</sup>. When the [United States Air Force (USAF)](/organisations/united-states-air-force-usaf) released the negative conclusions of the Condon Committee, French scientists and military personnel who had quietly accumulated their own UFO data were not convinced<sup>5</sup>. Some of these researchers joined UFO research groups in Paris, while others in high positions at the French Space Agency worked alone and secretly on the subject<sup>6</sup>; French scientists also obtained and carefully translated documents from the UMMO organization after hearing of them through private channels<sup>7</sup>.

According to the source, French scientists who tracked manoeuvrable, seemingly "impossible" objects responded by seeking to suppress their manifestation rather than investigating whether they represented advanced technology<sup>8</sup>, a pattern described as denying observations, blaming sightings on airplanes even against unassailable documentation, and destroying data once it was shown no airplane could account for the behaviour observed<sup>9</sup>. Against this backdrop, the Case of Dr. X, involving a medical doctor holding an important official position in southern France, became one of the most thoroughly investigated accounts of interaction between human percipients and the UFO phenomenon<sup>10</sup>.

A more formal, though still non-governmental, inquiry followed decades later with the COMETA Report. Some of the military officers on the COMETA panel served with the French Institute of Higher Studies for National Defense, a government-financed strategic planning agency<sup>11</sup>. The report itself was not a government-sanctioned study; it was undertaken independently by thirteen retired generals, scientists, and space experts working outside the French government, and then presented to the highest levels of government in France<sup>12</sup><sup>13</sup>. Its authors recommended that France establish sectorial cooperation agreements with other interested European and foreign countries on the matter of UFOs<sup>14</sup>.

France's twentieth-century history is also woven through the source material in other contexts. Great Britain and France issued an ultimatum to [Adolf Hitler](/people/adolf-hitler) demanding an end to all military action<sup>15</sup>, and when it was ignored, the two countries declared war on Germany, marking the official start of [World War II](/events/world-war-ii)<sup>16</sup>; a similar pattern had occurred at the outbreak of the First World War, when Germany's advance against France prompted Britain to declare war on the German Reich<sup>17</sup>. Separately, investigators traced Mehmet Ali Ağca's route through Germany, East Germany Germany, Hungary, Bulgaria, France, Spain, and Switzerland before his assassination attempt on John Paul II<sup>18</sup>, and Marcel Clément is identified as a French professor of philosophy<sup>19</sup>. The source also notes that Catholic priests at the end of the nineteenth century discouraged many French families from experimenting with tables believed to tap out messages from beyond<sup>20</sup>, and that, according to official documents, George Draper died in France on 1916-07-16<sup>21</sup>.
