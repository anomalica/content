The 2004 USS Nimitz UAP encounter is a series of military encounters involving [Unidentified Aerial Phenomena (UAP)](/topics/unidentified-aerial-phenomena-uap) detected by personnel of [Carrier Strike Group 11 (CSG-11)](/organisations/carrier-strike-group-11-csg-11) off the southern California coast during November 2004. From approximately 2004-11-10 to 2004-11-16, the strike group — including the aircraft carrier [USS Nimitz (CVN-68)](/objects/uss-nimitz-cvn-68) and the guided-missile cruiser [USS Princeton (CG-59)](/objects/uss-princeton-cg-59) — was conducting pre-deployment training in the Southern California Operating Area before deployment to the Arabian Sea.<sup>1</sup> During this period, the Princeton's radar operators detected multiple [anomalous aerial vehicle (Anomalous Aerial Vehicle (AAV))](/topics/anomalous-aerial-vehicle-anomalous-aerial-vehicle-aav) contacts operating in and around the vicinity of the strike group.<sup>2</sup> On the morning of 2004-11-14, [Kevin Day](/people/kevin-day), a radar operator and air intercept controller aboard the Princeton, observed a cluster of 14 unidentified objects on his screen and confirmed the [USS Nimitz (CVN-68)](/objects/uss-nimitz-cvn-68)'s radar was tracking the same contacts.<sup>3</sup> Radar data indicated the objects descended from approximately 24,000 metres to as low as approximately 15 metres above the ocean surface in approximately 0.78 of a second.<sup>4</sup> An [E-2 Hawkeye](/objects/e-2-hawkeye) airborne early-warning aircraft launched from the Nimitz also detected the closest contact on radar, providing additional corroboration.<sup>5</sup>

On 2004-11-14, the [USS Princeton (CG-59)](/objects/uss-princeton-cg-59) took control of a flight of [F/A-18](/objects/fa-18) aircraft returning from a training mission and vectored them in to investigate.<sup>6</sup> The flight comprised two [F/A-18F Super Hornet](/objects/fa-18f-super-hornet) aircraft, each crewed by a pilot and a weapons systems officer.<sup>7</sup> [David Fravor](/people/david-fravor), Commanding Officer of [Strike Fighter Squadron 41 (VFA-41)](/organisations/strike-fighter-squadron-41-vfa-41), piloted the lead aircraft, FastEagle 01.<sup>8</sup> [Alex Dietrich](/people/alex-dietrich) piloted the second aircraft, FastEagle 02.<sup>9</sup> The flight obtained both visual and sensor contact with the object at approximately N31 20' W117 10', about 130 kilometres south of the US/Mexico border and 56 kilometres off the Baja Mexico coast.<sup>10</sup> Conditions were clear with no clouds and five to ten knots of wind.<sup>11</sup> Despite the calm sea state, the crew observed a cross-shaped area of white water on the surface, described as approximately the size of a Boeing 737, with the appearance of a submerged object causing a wave-break beneath the surface.<sup>12</sup>

Above the disturbed water, the crews observed a white, featureless object. The official incident report described the [AAV](/objects/aav) as an elongated egg or Tic Tac shape with a discernible midline horizontal axis,<sup>13</sup> approximately 14 metres in length,<sup>14</sup> solid white, smooth, with no edges, no wings, no visible propulsion, no windows, and no visible probes.<sup>15</sup> The object moved in abrupt north-south and east-west motions with no apparent inertia.<sup>16</sup> Fravor descended at approximately 560 kilometres per hour to investigate,<sup>17</sup> closing to approximately 800 metres from the object.<sup>18</sup> As he moved to intercept, the object accelerated and disappeared in less than half a second.<sup>19</sup> [Jim Slaight](/people/jim-slaight), weapons systems officer in the second aircraft, described the departure: "There was no gradual acceleration or spooling up period, it just shot out of sight immediately."<sup>20</sup> Simultaneously, the [Water Disturbance](/objects/water-disturbance) on the ocean surface had also vanished.<sup>21</sup> The entire visual encounter lasted approximately five minutes, observed on a clear day by four trained military aviators.<sup>22</sup> Shortly after, the [USS Princeton (CG-59)](/objects/uss-princeton-cg-59) radioed Fravor's crew to report that a UAP had reappeared at their prearranged combat air patrol point — a location that had not been broadcast on any open channel.<sup>23</sup>

Following Fravor's return, [Chad Underwood](/people/chad-underwood), a member of [Strike Fighter Squadron 41 (VFA-41)](/organisations/strike-fighter-squadron-41-vfa-41), flew a subsequent sortie carrying an [ATFLIR Pod](/objects/atflir-pod) electro-optical targeting system to pursue the object.<sup>24</sup> The ATFLIR's infrared mode showed the object as a white form against a black background with no sign of jet exhaust or propulsive gases.<sup>25</sup> When Underwood attempted to obtain a radar lock, the system indicated it was being jammed.<sup>26</sup> Underwood later stated the object was travelling from approximately 15,000 metres to approximately 30 metres in seconds, which he described as not possible.<sup>27</sup> The [F/A-18F Super Hornet](/objects/fa-18f-super-hornet) radar could not obtain a lock on the object when it was manoeuvring, though it could be tracked when stationary using forward-looking infrared (FLIR).<sup>28</sup> The resulting footage, subsequently referred to as the [Tic Tac UAP Full Video](/documents/tic-tac-uap-full-video), constitutes the primary sensor record of the encounter. The key assessments section of the official incident report concluded that the object exhibited advanced aerodynamic performance with no visible control surfaces and no visible means to generate lift,<sup>29</sup> and was assessed as no known aircraft in the inventory of the [United States Navy](/organisations/united-states-navy) or any foreign nation.<sup>30</sup>

In the weeks following the encounter, the aircrew involved experienced a degree of ridicule within the carrier group.<sup>31</sup> The incident received limited public attention until December 2017, when [The New York Times](/organisations/the-new-york-times) published a report — the [2017 NYT Pentagon UAP Article](/documents/2017-nyt-pentagon-uap-article) — disclosing the existence of a government programme, the Advanced Aerospace Threat Identification Program (AATIP), associated with [Luis Elizondo](/people/luis-elizondo), alongside the release of sensor footage from the encounter. The publication prompted a Navy commander to contact Fravor about a Freedom of Information Act request for materials relating to the incident.<sup>32</sup> [The United States Navy](/organisations/united-states-navy) subsequently confirmed the footage as genuine and stated the phenomena depicted remained unidentified. As of the time of reporting, the US government has stated it does not know what the object observed during the encounter was.<sup>33</sup>
