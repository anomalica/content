The 2004 USS Nimitz UAP encounter refers to a series of radar detections and a visual intercept involving pilots of Carrier Strike Group 11 (CSG-11), centred on 2004-11-14. According to the Nimitz Carrier Strike Group (CSG-11) AAV Incident Report, the strike group built around the [USS Nimitz (CVN-68)](/objects/uss-nimitz-cvn-68) was operating off the western coast of the United States between approximately 2004-11-10 and 2004-11-16, in preparation for deployment to the Arabian Sea.<sup>1</sup> Journalist Ross Coulthart writes that on 14 November 2004 the Nimitz was conducting preparatory training approximately 100 kilometres off the coast between San Diego and Ensenada, Mexico, ahead of deployment to the Middle East.<sup>2</sup> In the days beforehand, the incident report states, the [USS Princeton (CG-59)](/objects/uss-princeton-cg-59) on several occasions detected multiple [Anomalous Aerial Vehicles (AAVs)](/topics/anomalous-aerial-vehicle-anomalous-aerial-vehicle-aav) operating in and around the vicinity of the carrier strike group,<sup>3</sup> descending very rapidly from approximately 18,000 metres to approximately 15 metres in a matter of seconds,<sup>4</sup> then hovering or remaining stationary on radar for a short time before departing again at high velocities and turn rates.<sup>5</sup> [Kevin Day](/people/kevin-day), a radar operator aboard the Princeton, said he was told by the ship's captain that the objects had been tracked for days before the 14 November encounter.<sup>6</sup>

On 2004-11-14, after again detecting an AAV, the Princeton directed a flight of two returning F/A-18Fs to investigate further, according to the incident report.<sup>7</sup> [David Fravor](/people/david-fravor) said his flight comprised four aircrew, two crew to each of two F/A-18F jets,<sup>8</sup> and had launched on a training mission that was then cancelled and redirected.<sup>9</sup> Flying as "Blue Air" in two [F/A-18F Super Hornet](/objects/fa-18f-super-hornet)s, the flight was under the control of the Princeton.<sup>10</sup> Fravor and [Alex Dietrich](/people/alex-dietrich), each with a weapons systems officer in the back seat, were diverted to investigate the AAVs, according to CBS News.<sup>11</sup> Fravor said the Princeton, an Aegis cruiser, vectored the flight through a single controller who first asked what ordnance they were carrying,<sup>12</sup> then told them training had been suspended for real-world tasking;<sup>13</sup> he and his wingman proceeded toward a contact west of their combat air patrol point, roughly 64 kilometres south of the ship.<sup>14</sup> The incident report states that the Princeton took over control of the jets from an [E-2 Hawkeye](/objects/e-2-hawkeye) (E-2C) airborne early warning aircraft and vectored them in for the intercept, bringing the crews to visual contact approximately 1.6 kilometres from the AAV.<sup>15</sup>

Fravor and Dietrich found an area of roiling white water the size of a Boeing 737 in an otherwise calm sea, according to CBS News.<sup>16</sup> Above it, Fravor described a featureless, gleaming white object with two appendages beneath its belly, hovering just above the ocean surface, with no windows, no visible engines, no wings, no exhaust and no markings.<sup>17</sup> As he descended toward it, the object began mirroring his aircraft's movements, climbing as he descended and tracking his [F/A-18](/objects/fa-18), he said.<sup>18</sup> [Jim Slaight](/people/jim-slaight), the weapons systems officer in the second jet, said the object had defined edges but a fuzzy-looking border, like heat waves rising from a hot paved road.<sup>19</sup> When Fravor pulled his aircraft's nose onto the object at a range of approximately 0.8 kilometres, it rapidly accelerated and disappeared, and his wingman, roughly 2,400 metres above, also lost visual contact, Fravor told the House Oversight Committee.<sup>20</sup> He said the object travelled approximately 97 kilometres in less than a minute,<sup>21</sup> before reappearing on the Princeton's AN/SPY-1 radar at the combat air patrol point, roughly 97 kilometres to the east.<sup>22</sup> Fravor later described the acceleration: "I cut across the circle and as I get within about a half mile of it, it rapidly accelerates to the south in about two seconds and disappears . . . well above supersonic. Like a bullet out of a gun, it took off."<sup>23</sup>

Fravor said there was no video from his own encounter, since all four aircrew observed the object with their own eyes, but that [Chad Underwood](/people/chad-underwood) obtained video of an object on a later flight.<sup>24</sup> When Fravor's crew saw Underwood's footage, they confirmed it matched exactly what they had seen.<sup>25</sup> Underwood later told New York Magazine that the object he filmed "wasn't behaving by normal laws of physics."<sup>26</sup> Fravor told the House Oversight Committee that a radar tape showing jamming of the AN/APG-73 radar in his aircraft was not part of the video later released to the public.<sup>27</sup> According to Ross Coulthart, Underwood's weapons systems officer located the object at the combat air patrol point using the ATFLIR Pod, and there were clear indications of deliberate radar jamming when the crew tried to lock onto it.<sup>28</sup>

The encounter went unreported for years. According to CBS News, after the aircrew filed their reports, nothing was said or done officially for five years, until [Luis Elizondo](/people/luis-elizondo) came across the case and investigated.<sup>29</sup> In August 2017 the Advanced Aerospace Threat Identification Program (AATIP) released a video of a whitish oval object, about the size of a commercial plane, chased by two Navy F/A-18F fighter jets from the Nimitz off the coast of San Diego, according to reporting in The New York Times.<sup>30</sup> The object became known as "the Tic Tac", and the case is more formally referred to as the USS Nimitz [UFO](/topics/unidentified-flying-object-ufo) incident, according to podcast host Lex Fridman.<sup>31</sup>

The CSG-11 incident report assessed that the AAV took evasive action on intercept by the F/A-18, demonstrating advanced acceleration, aerodynamic and propulsion capability,<sup>32</sup> and concluded that it was no known aircraft or air vehicle currently in the inventory of the United States or any foreign nation.<sup>33</sup> The report also assessed that, based on the absence of any unidentified sonar contacts, it was highly unlikely that the AAV had operated below the ocean's surface, though it noted that the AAV might have demonstrated an ability to be cloaked or invisible to the human eye, based on pilot reports of a water disturbance with no visible craft.<sup>34</sup>
