The 2004 USS Nimitz UAP encounter, part of the study of Unidentified Anomalous Phenomena (UAP), refers to a series of radar and visual contacts between US Navy fighter aircraft and an object subsequently termed an Anomalous Aerial Vehicle (AAV) in the incident report produced afterwards, the case now catalogued under the [anomalous aerial vehicle (Anomalous Aerial Vehicle (AAV))](/topics/anomalous-aerial-vehicle-anomalous-aerial-vehicle-aav) topic. According to the Nimitz Carrier Strike Group (CSG-11) AAV Incident Report, the Carrier Strike Group 11 (CSG-11) was operating off the western coast of the United States between approximately 2004-11-10 and 2004-11-16 in preparation for deployment to the Arabian Sea.<sup>1</sup> The report situates the broader sequence of events in the Southern California (SOCAL) Operating Area off the coast of California and Mexico during November and December 2004.<sup>2</sup> The only units the report names as participants in the detection and intercept are the [USS Princeton (CG-59)](/objects/uss-princeton-cg-59), Carrier Airborne Early Warning Squadron 117 (VAW-117), Marine Fighter Attack Squadron 232 (VMFA-232), and [Strike Fighter Squadron 41 (VFA-41)](/organisations/strike-fighter-squadron-41-vfa-41).<sup>3</sup>

The report states that the AAVs would descend rapidly from approximately 18,000 metres to approximately 15 metres within seconds,<sup>4</sup> hovering or remaining stationary on radar for a short time before departing at high velocities and turn rates.<sup>5</sup> On 2004-11-14, after again detecting an AAV, the USS Princeton directed a flight of two [F/A-18Fs](/objects/fa-18f-super-hornet) returning from a training mission to investigate further.<sup>6</sup> The report identifies [David Fravor](/people/david-fravor), Commanding Officer of VFA-41, as the pilot of FastEagle 01, the lead aircraft of the first F/A-18F section airborne that day,<sup>7</sup> with [Alex Dietrich](/people/alex-dietrich) piloting FastEagle 02.<sup>8</sup> The flight carried two captive training AIM-9M Sidewinders and no other ordnance.<sup>9</sup> The USS Princeton took over control of the F/A-18s from the [E-2C Hawkeye](/objects/e-2-hawkeye) airborne early warning aircraft and vectored them in for an intercept, resulting in visual contact approximately 1.6 kilometres from the AAV.<sup>10</sup> The report places this contact at approximately N31°20' W117°10', about 130 kilometres south of the US/Mexico border and 56 kilometres off the Baja Mexico coast.<sup>11</sup>

According to the report, the AAV was an elongated egg or "Tic Tac" shape with a discernible midline horizontal axis,<sup>12</sup> solid white, smooth, with no edges, and approximately 14 metres in length.<sup>13</sup> Upon intercept by the [F/A-18](/objects/fa-18), it took evasive action that the report describes as demonstrating advanced acceleration, aerodynamic, and propulsion capability,<sup>14</sup> though it did not take any offensive action against the strike group.<sup>15</sup> When Fravor's flight returned to the carrier and proceeded to the Carrier Intelligence Center, personnel there had donned tin-foil caps and asked about the "[UFO](/topics/unidentified-flying-object-ufo) flight".<sup>16</sup>

A second F/A-18F section, in which [Chad Underwood](/people/chad-underwood) flew as Weapons and Sensors Officer, launched following the FastEagle flight.<sup>17</sup> The report notes that the F/A-18Fs' radar could not obtain a lock on the AAV, though it could be tracked while stationary and at slower speeds using forward-looking infrared (FLIR).<sup>18</sup> Aircrew aboard the E-2C monitored the Air Defense Control Net during the contact, heard merge calls typical of an airborne intercept of an enemy fighter, and concluded that the fighters were taking the contact seriously.<sup>19</sup> The USS Princeton retained control of the F/A-18s for the duration of the contact and intercept, while the E-2C remained airborne but was no longer involved.<sup>20</sup> On several occasions, the report states, the USS Princeton detected multiple AAVs operating in and around the vicinity of the strike group.<sup>21</sup>

The report's Key Assessments section concludes that the AAV was no known aircraft or air vehicle in the inventory of the United States or any foreign nation,<sup>22</sup> and that it exhibited advanced propulsion capability, remaining stationary with little to no variation in altitude before transitioning to horizontal and/or vertical velocities far greater than any known aerial vehicle with little to no visible signature.<sup>23</sup> It also assesses advanced low-observable characteristics at multiple radar bands, rendering US radar-based engagement capabilities ineffective,<sup>24</sup> and advanced aerodynamic performance with no visible control surfaces and no visible means to generate lift.<sup>25</sup> The report further states that the AAV possibly demonstrated a highly advanced capability to operate undersea, completely undetectable by the most advanced sensors.<sup>26</sup> Elsewhere, however, it concludes that, based on the lack of detection of any unidentified sonar contacts, it is highly unlikely that an AAV operated below the surface of the ocean during the encounter, while noting it is possible the AAV demonstrated an ability to be cloaked or invisible to the human eye, based on pilot reporting of water disturbance with no visible craft.<sup>27</sup> The former commander of the submarine USS Louisville (SSN-724) confirmed there was no anomalous undersea activity during the period of the encounter.<sup>28</sup> Separately, the report assesses that the AAV possibly demonstrated the ability to cloak or become invisible to the human eye or human observation.<sup>29</sup>

According to the report, the aircrew involved experienced a high level of ridicule in the weeks that followed.<sup>30</sup> The 3rd Fleet Intelligence Officer (N2) at the time of the encounter has since retired from the [United States Navy (USN)](/organisations/united-states-navy-usn) and could not be located.<sup>31</sup> Admiral Peter Daly, who assumed command of the strike group in January 2005, had no knowledge of or involvement in the encounter, according to the report.<sup>32</sup> In August 2017, the Advanced Aerospace Threat Identification Program (AATIP) released a video of a whitish oval object, about the size of a commercial plane, chased by two Navy F/A-18F fighter jets from the aircraft carrier [USS Nimitz (CVN-68)](/objects/uss-nimitz-cvn-68) off the coast of San Diego in 2004.<sup>33</sup>
