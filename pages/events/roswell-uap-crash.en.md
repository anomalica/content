The Roswell UAP Crash refers to an incident in July 1947 in which debris was recovered from a crashed object near Roswell, New Mexico, USA. On 5 July 1947, ranch foreman Mac Brazel found crash debris scattered across a cattle ranch approximately 120 kilometres from Roswell.<sup>1</sup> [Jesse Marcel](/people/jesse-marcel), the intelligence officer dispatched to the site, described a massive field of debris approximately 1,200 metres long and 60 to 90 metres wide, with a gouge extending up to 150 metres long.<sup>2</sup> On 8 July 1947, Colonel William Blanchard ordered [Walter Haut](/people/walter-haut) to issue a press release announcing that the US Army had recovered a flying disc.<sup>3</sup> The resulting [Roswell 1947 Press Release](/objects/roswell-1947-press-release) stated that the intelligence office of the 509th Bomb Group had gained possession of a disc,<sup>4</sup> and the *Roswell Daily Record* ran the headline "RAAF Captures Flying Saucer on Ranch in Roswell Region".<sup>5</sup> Within hours, General Ramey issued a counter-statement asserting the recovered object was simply a weather balloon,<sup>6</sup> a retraction that [David Fravor](/people/david-fravor) later noted followed within a day of the initial flying saucer announcement.<sup>7</sup>

Controversy over the official explanation persisted for decades, driven in part by testimony from those present at the time. [Jesse Marcel](/people/jesse-marcel) later claimed that the real debris he had recovered was substituted by General Ramey with wreckage from a weather balloon, and that he personally believed what had crashed was extraterrestrial in origin.<sup>8</sup> In 1978, Marcel told researcher [Stanton Friedman](/people/stanton-friedman) of his convictions, prompting Friedman to allege what he termed a "cosmic Watergate cover-up".<sup>9</sup> The US Air Force's [Roswell Air Force 1994 Report](/documents/roswell-air-force-1994-report) later acknowledged that the original weather balloon account was false, attributing the wreckage instead to the classified [Project MOGUL](/matters/project-mogul) — a programme designed to monitor Soviet nuclear tests via high-altitude balloons.<sup>10</sup> The [Roswell Report Fact vs Fiction 1995](/documents/roswell-report-fact-vs-fiction-1995) reiterated that no information had been located indicating the incident involved a UFO, and that recovered materials were consistent with a Project Mogul balloon.<sup>11</sup> A further USAF report in 1997 provided additional materials in support of the same conclusion.<sup>12</sup> The [Government Accountability Office](/organisations/government-accountability-office) found in 1995 that four air accidents were reported by the US Army Air Force in New Mexico during July 1947, all occurring after 8 July 1947.<sup>13</sup> Separately, the same review found that not a single document from Roswell Army Airfield covering the crash period had survived — every document had been destroyed with no apparent authorisation.<sup>14</sup> President Bill Clinton, who had pushed for that review, acknowledged publicly that many mysteries surrounding Roswell remained.<sup>15</sup>

[Walter Haut](/people/walter-haut), who had authored the original 1947 press release at Blanchard's direction, provided further testimony decades later. In the [Walter Haut Roswell Affidavit 2002](/documents/walter-haut-roswell-affidavit-2002), signed on 2002-12-26, Haut stated he was taken by Colonel Blanchard to a hangar where he viewed recovered child-sized bodies with abnormally large heads lying under a tarpaulin.<sup>16</sup> The affidavit also described an apparent craft approximately 3.6 to 4.6 metres in length, roughly 1.8 metres high, and egg-shaped.<sup>17</sup> Researcher [Don Schmitt](/people/don-schmitt) stated that the affidavit was drafted by himself and his co-author based on information Haut had provided over the years, and that Haut read and approved every word in the presence of his daughter, a notary, and a witness.<sup>18</sup> Official investigations concluded separately that claims of alien bodies were most likely the result of conflation with a 1956 KC-97 aircraft accident and a 1959 manned balloon mishap,<sup>19</sup> and that so-called alien bodies reported in the New Mexico desert were test dummies carried aloft by high-altitude balloons for scientific research.<sup>20</sup>

A number of competing alternative hypotheses have been advanced outside official channels. [Annie Jacobsen](/people/annie-jacobsen), in a 2011 history of Area 51, asserted that what crashed at Roswell was Russian in origin, built by captured Nazi scientists.<sup>21</sup> [Tom DeLonge](/people/tom-delonge) advanced a similar claim in his [DeLonge Joe Rogan Interview](/events/delonge-joe-rogan-interview), also describing the craft as a Nazi-built Russian flying saucer.<sup>22</sup> By contrast, [Harold E. Puthoff](/people/harold-e-puthoff) told [Luis Elizondo](/people/luis-elizondo) that the Roswell crash of 1947 was a genuine event in which four deceased nonhuman bodies were recovered.<sup>23</sup> Puthoff also presented the [Roswell EMP Crash Hypothesis](/concepts/roswell-emp-crash-hypothesis), proposing that the crash was caused by [Electromagnetic Pulse](/concepts/electromagnetic-pulse) energy — a by-product of atomic bomb testing at a nearby test range.<sup>24</sup> This hypothesis was further elaborated with the claim that the UAP may have been conducting reconnaissance on US atomic testing when an EMP caused it to crash,<sup>25</sup> and that the craft came down near a government test facility and broke into two crash sites.<sup>26</sup>

Claims regarding physical evidence and recovered material have also featured in witness accounts. According to the Art's Parts letters, alleged crash debris included corpses of alien occupants that were sent to [Wright Patterson Air Force Base](/places/wright-patterson-air-force-base).<sup>27</sup> [Harold E. Puthoff](/people/harold-e-puthoff) was reported to be in possession of material allegedly recovered from the Roswell crash, described as an intricate and fragile piece with multiple microscopic layers of interlacing bismuth and magnesium.<sup>28</sup> [Luis Elizondo](/people/luis-elizondo) noted that the government revised its account of the Roswell incident at least twice in the decades following the crash.<sup>29</sup> [Edgar Mitchell](/people/edgar-mitchell), whose hometown of Artesia, New Mexico, was near the crash site, reportedly confided to [Harold E. Puthoff](/people/harold-e-puthoff) and [Eric Davis](/people/eric-davis) that his family was among Roswell-area families threatened by the [FBI](/organisations/fbi) after the incident, with agents visiting ranchers to warn that speaking about the crashes could cost them their lives.<sup>30</sup>
