The All-domain Anomaly Resolution Office (AARO) is an office within the [United States Department of Defense (DoD)](/organisations/united-states-department-of-defense-dod) responsible for detecting, identifying, and characterising [Unidentified Anomalous Phenomena](/topics/unidentified-anomalous-phenomena-uap) (UAP) across all domains, including air, space, subsurface, and transmedium environments. The office was formally established on 2022-07-20,<sup>1</sup> replacing and expanding upon the Airborne Object Identification and Management Synchronization Group (AOIMSG), which had not achieved initial operating capability before being superseded.<sup>4</sup> The National Defense Authorization Act for Fiscal Year 2022 provided the legislative basis for AARO's creation,<sup>2</sup> and the Deputy Secretary of Defense, acting in coordination with the Director of National Intelligence, directed the [Office of the Under Secretary of Defense for Intelligence (OUSDI)](/organisations/office-of-the-under-secretary-of-defense-for-intelligence-ousdi) to rename AOIMSG as AARO and expand its scope, effective 2022-07-15.<sup>5</sup> The office also absorbed the investigation remit previously held by the Unidentified Aerial Phenomena Task Force, extending coverage to objects operating underwater.<sup>13</sup>

AARO sits under the [Office of the Under Secretary of Defense for Intelligence (OUSDI)](/organisations/office-of-the-under-secretary-of-defense-for-intelligence-ousdi) and is charged with synchronising efforts across the DoD and with other federal departments and agencies to detect, identify, and characterise objects of interest in, on, or near military installations, operating areas, training areas, special use airspace, and other areas of interest, and, as necessary, to mitigate any associated threats to safety of operations and national security.<sup>6</sup> The National Defense Authorization Act for Fiscal Year 2022 assigned AARO responsibility for synchronising and standardising the collection, analysis, and identification of UAP incidents,<sup>7</sup> including developing processes to ensure incidents are reported in a centralised database and coordinating with allies and partners.<sup>8</sup> AARO organised itself around four internal functions: analysis, operations, science and technology, and strategic communications.<sup>9</sup> The [National Defense Authorization Act for Fiscal Year 2023](/documents/national-defense-authorization-act-for-fiscal-year-2023) added a requirement for AARO to establish a secure reporting mechanism through which individuals could report UAP incidents without being subject to a nondisclosure agreement or reprisal.<sup>10</sup> AARO provides quarterly reports, semiannual briefings, and an annual report to [United States Congress](/organisations/united-states-congress) in coordination with the [Office of the Director of National Intelligence (ODNI)](/organisations/office-of-the-director-of-national-intelligence-odni);<sup>11</sup> on 2023-01-12, the [Office of the Director of National Intelligence (ODNI)](/organisations/office-of-the-director-of-national-intelligence-odni) submitted the 2022 Annual Report on Unidentified Aerial Phenomena to Congress, drafted in partnership with AARO and based on AARO's data.<sup>12</sup>

Sean Kirkpatrick served as the first Director of AARO, taking up the position in July 2022. [David Charles Grusch](/people/david-charles-grusch), who had served as his agency's co-lead in [Unidentified Anomalous Phenomena (UAP)](/topics/unidentified-anomalous-phenomena-uap) and trans-medium object analysis reporting to the Unidentified Anomalous Phenomena Task Force and later to AARO, held a classified conversation with Kirkpatrick in April 2022, prior to Kirkpatrick assuming the directorship, in which Grusch provided national-security concerns.<sup>22</sup> Kirkpatrick subsequently testified before the Senate in 2023 that AARO had found no evidence of extraterrestrial activity,<sup>15</sup> and testified before Congress that there had been no credible evidence of extraterrestrial activity or off-world technology brought to the attention of the office.<sup>16</sup> Grusch publicly disputed this assessment, stating that some of the roughly 30 individuals who had come to AARO were also interviewed by him, and that he was able to evaluate what they had provided.<sup>23</sup> [Marco Rubio](/people/marco-rubio) separately expressed concern that the [United States Department of Defense (DoD)](/organisations/united-states-department-of-defense-dod) had not been sharing information on hundreds of UAP with the investigators and scientists at AARO.<sup>20</sup> An initial AARO analysis reported 171 uncharacterised UAP reports that appeared to have demonstrated unusual flight characteristics or performance capabilities.<sup>17</sup> AARO's budget remained classified, which observers noted prohibited meaningful oversight from [United States Congress](/organisations/united-states-congress).<sup>21</sup>

In 2024-02-01, AARO published Volume I of its All-domain Anomaly Resolution Office (AARO) Historical Record Report (HR2), covering the record of [United States Intelligence Community](/organisations/united-states-intelligence-community) and DoD involvement with [Unidentified Flying Object (UFO)](/topics/unidentified-flying-object-ufo)/UAP from 1945 to 2023-10-31.<sup>24</sup> For the report, AARO reviewed official US Government efforts since 1945 and identified approximately two dozen separate official investigative efforts.<sup>25</sup> As part of its research, AARO partnered with the National Archives and Records Administration (NARA) to examine records from the [United States Air Force (USAF)](/organisations/united-states-air-force-usaf)'s [Project Blue Book](/projects/project-blue-book), and partnered with archives of key intelligence and national security agencies including the [Central Intelligence Agency (CIA)](/organisations/central-intelligence-agency-cia), Department of Energy, and [National Geospatial-Intelligence Agency (NGA)](/organisations/national-geospatial-intelligence-agency-nga). As of 2023-09-17, AARO had interviewed approximately 30 individuals who claimed to have insight into alleged US Government involvement in off-world technology exploitation or UAP incidents near nuclear facilities. The Report on the Historical Record of U.S. Government Involvement with Unidentified Anomalous Phenomena (UAP), Volume I found no empirical evidence that any UAP investigatory effort since 1945 had uncovered verifiable information regarding the recovery or existence of extraterrestrial beings or craft.<sup>26</sup> AARO also examined a physical sample claimed to originate from an extraterrestrial craft and, in conjunction with a leading science laboratory, concluded it was a manufactured terrestrial metallic alloy, possibly of [United States Air Force (USAF)](/organisations/united-states-air-force-usaf) origin.<sup>27</sup> AARO sent guidance and requests to the DoD, [United States Intelligence Community](/organisations/united-states-intelligence-community), Department of Energy, and [Department of Homeland Security (DHS)](/organisations/department-of-homeland-security-dhs) to review any nondisclosure agreements pertaining to UAP, and discovered no agreements containing UAP-specific threats to interviewees.

The Evaluation of the DoD's Actions Regarding Unidentified Anomalous Phenomena (DODIG-2023-109), produced by the U.S. Department of Defense Office of Inspector General, examined AARO's early operations and concluded that the combatant commands would be the logical organisations to detect, report, collect, and identify UAP incidents to AARO, and recommended that the Under Secretary of Defense for Intelligence and Security issue a DoD policy integrating UAP roles, responsibilities, and coordination procedures into existing intelligence and counterintelligence policies.<sup>30</sup> The report also noted that the military services and components had sent some UAP incident reports to AARO but that the DoD did not formally require them to do so. AARO concurred with the report's principal recommendation<sup>31</sup> and indicated it would achieve full operational capability using the resources provided in the Future Year Defense Plan beginning in Fiscal Year 2024. AARO assessed that the classification of prior US Government investigations had fuelled public speculation that the government was concealing knowledge of extraterrestrials, while secrecy was in fact intended to protect sensitive military and intelligence community programmes, capabilities, sources, and methods.<sup>28</sup> The office further assessed that modern allegations of hidden reverse-engineering of extraterrestrial technology largely originated from a consistent group of individuals with ties to the cancelled [Advanced Aerospace Weapon Systems Applications Program (Advanced Aerospace Weapon System Applications Program (Advanced Aerospace Weapon Systems Applications Program (AAWSAP)))](/projects/advanced-aerospace-weapon-systems-applications-program-advanced-aerospace-weapon-system-applications-program-advanced-aerospace-weapon-systems-applications-program-aawsap) and associated private sector paranormal research efforts.<sup>29</sup> The [United States Army](/organisations/united-states-army) and The White House were among the additional entities whose records and personnel AARO engaged in the course of its historical investigations.
