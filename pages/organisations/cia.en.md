The Central Intelligence Agency has been linked to the [Unidentified Anomalous Phenomena](/concepts/unidentified-anomalous-phenomena) subject since at least the early 1950s, when a surge in UFO sightings prompted internal review. Following a notable wave of sightings over Washington D.C. in the summer of 1952, the CIA's Deputy Director for Intelligence tasked the Office of Scientific Intelligence's Physics and Electronics Division to assess UFO cases.<sup>1</sup> By December 1952, [H. Marshall Chadwell](/people/h-marshall-chadwell), Assistant Director of the Office of Scientific Intelligence, had briefed Director of Central Intelligence Walter Bedell Smith on the matter.<sup>2</sup> Smith had reportedly already been privately advised that UAP sightings raised serious national security concerns and were "not attributable to natural phenomenon or known types of aerial vehicles".<sup>3</sup>

The most consequential early CIA action on the UFO question was the convening of the [Robertson Panel](/matters/robertson-panel), the Scientific Advisory Panel on Unidentified Flying Objects, which met from 14 to 18 January 1953 under the auspices of the CIA Office of Scientific Intelligence.<sup>4</sup> The panel reportedly advised the Air Force to enlist the Walt Disney Company and mass media in a campaign to debunk UAP, ostensibly to prevent US air defence communications from being overwhelmed by public reports.<sup>5</sup> The AARO Historical Record Report later confirmed there was at least one USG proposal — by the CIA-sponsored Robertson Panel — to engage in an active debunking effort using public media tools.<sup>6</sup> One analysis characterised the broader climate of hostility toward the UAP issue as a direct result of US government policies formulated by the panel.<sup>7</sup> The CIA updated its UFO evaluation in 1964 following White House discussions, with Director of Central Intelligence John McCone tasking the agency with a fresh assessment;<sup>8</sup> at that time, the CIA's scientific division also formally acquired UFO-sighting case information from [NICAP](/organisations/nicap), a private organisation.<sup>9</sup> Separately, a declassified CIA assessment noted that more than half of UFO reports investigated during the 1950s and 1960s were assessed to be US reconnaissance flights,<sup>10</sup> a finding connected to CIA-led programmes such as the OXCART programme, approved by [Dwight D. Eisenhower](/people/dwight-d-eisenhower) in 1958 to develop a successor to the [U-2 Dragon Lady Aircraft](/objects/u-2-dragon-lady-aircraft).<sup>11</sup> UFO reports were documented to spike when the U-2 was in flight, particularly from airline pilots reporting to Air Traffic Control.<sup>12</sup>

The CIA also sponsored the [Stargate Program](/matters/stargate-program), the federal government's remote-viewing research effort. The programme was run for years by the CIA before later transferring to the Defense Intelligence Agency (DIA).<sup>13</sup> [Harold E. Puthoff](/people/harold-e-puthoff), then a [Stanford University](/organisations/stanford-university) researcher and employee of the [National Security Agency](/organisations/national-security-agency), was approached by the CIA along with his colleague [Russell Targ](/people/russell-targ), after the CIA informed them that the Soviet Union had its own remote-viewing programme and that the US needed to develop a counterpart capability.<sup>14</sup><sup>15</sup> The programme reportedly became sufficiently successful that Puthoff reported directly to the CIA director and the White House on a regular basis.<sup>16</sup>

The CIA's relationship with UAP-related political developments continued across subsequent administrations. According to [Daniel Sheehan](/people/daniel-sheehan), President [Jimmy Carter](/people/jimmy-carter) sought a UAP briefing from CIA Director George Herbert Walker Bush prior to his January 1977 inauguration, but was refused on the grounds that he had no need to know.<sup>17</sup> In 1993, President Bill Clinton ordered CIA Director [James Woolsey](/people/james-woolsey) to release more files on UAPs following lobbying from Laurance Rockefeller.<sup>18</sup> That same year, [Steven Greer](/people/steven-greer) has claimed to have held a covert briefing on UFOs with Woolsey, conducted under the guise of a dinner party.<sup>19</sup> In 1986, following the JAL 1628 sighting, CIA agents reportedly confiscated all radar evidence at a meeting held at the Federal Aviation Administration's Washington office, according to [John Callahan](/people/john-callahan);<sup>20</sup> Callahan further stated that an order was made by the CIA to deny the meeting had ever taken place.<sup>21</sup>

Several individuals closely associated with [To the Stars Academy of Arts and Science](/organisations/to-the-stars-academy-of-arts-and-science) (TTSA) held significant CIA backgrounds. [Jim Semivan](/people/jim-semivan) joined the CIA in 1982 and retired in 2007 after a twenty-five-year career as an operations officer, at which point he was a member of the CIA's Senior Intelligence Service, before remaining a CIA contractor after retirement.<sup>22</sup><sup>23</sup> [Norm Kahn](/people/norm-kahn), a TTSA advisory board member, was a former CIA expert on biological weapons decorated with the CIA's Intelligence Medal.<sup>24</sup> During the [Pentagon Advanced Aerospace Threat Identification Program (AATIP) UFO Program, 2007–2017](/matters/20072017-pentagon-advanced-aerospace-threat-identification-program-aatip-ufo-program), [Luis Elizondo](/people/luis-elizondo) worked with CIA officials after 2012<sup>25</sup> and led classified investigations with partners including the CIA and [FBI](/organisations/fbi).<sup>26</sup> The [National Security Agency](/organisations/national-security-agency) and CIA offered to provide assets for the UAP-related operation known as OPLAN Interloper, with weekly meetings subsequently convened in Sensitive Compartmented Information Facility (SCIF) spaces at the Pentagon and other agencies.<sup>27</sup> Elizondo also convened a meeting at which the [Gimbal Video](/documents/gimbal-video) was screened before CIA, NSA, and Navy representatives, none of whom believed the object depicted was of human manufacture.<sup>28</sup><sup>29</sup>

Former CIA Director John Brennan has publicly stated that he found some UAP videos "eyebrow-raising" and acknowledged that certain phenomena could potentially involve a different form of life.<sup>30</sup> The CIA's institutional posture toward UAP has thus ranged from active debunking policy in the 1950s, through sponsorship of research programmes such as Stargate, to more recent engagement with UAP data alongside defence and intelligence partners.
