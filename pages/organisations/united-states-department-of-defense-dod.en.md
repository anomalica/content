The United States Department of Defense (DoD) has relied heavily on the military services and their components to detect, report, collect, analyse, and identify Unidentified Anomalous Phenomena (UAP) since the 1940s.<sup>1</sup> Its formal re-engagement with the subject began in the mid-2000s, when members of the [United States Congress](/organisations/united-states-congress) initiated and funded the [Advanced Aerospace Weapon Systems Applications Program (AAWSAP)](/projects/advanced-aerospace-weapon-systems-applications-program-advanced-aerospace-weapon-system-applications-program-advanced-aerospace-weapon-systems-applications-program-aawsap) to study UAP under the direction of the Defense Intelligence Agency (DIA).<sup>2</sup> Unlike AAWSAP, the [Advanced Aerospace Threat Identification Program (AATIP)](/organisations/advanced-aerospace-threat-identification-program-aatip) was never an official DoD programme; after AAWSAP was cancelled, the AATIP name was used by individuals associated with an informal, unofficial UAP community of interest within the department.<sup>3</sup> According to AARO's Historical Record Report, on 2009-06-24 Senator [Harry Reid](/people/harry-reid) wrote to then Deputy Secretary of Defense William Lynn III requesting that AAWSAP/AATIP be designated a DoD [Special Access Program (SAP)](/topics/special-access-program-sap);<sup>4</sup> Lynn declined, the report states, on the recommendation of then Under Secretary of Defense for Intelligence James Clapper that the designation was not justified.<sup>5</sup> AAWSAP/AATIP was terminated in 2012 amid DIA and DoD concerns about the project.<sup>6</sup>

According to [The New York Times](/organisations/the-new-york-times), AATIP investigated reports of [UFOs](/topics/unidentified-flying-object-ufo) for years, drawing on interviews with programme participants and government records.<sup>7</sup> Within DoD's roughly $600 billion annual budget, the programme's $22 million in funding was almost impossible to find.<sup>8</sup> According to George Knapp, internal documents described a number of incidents in which UFOs demonstrated intense interest in US military assets, particularly nuclear weapons.<sup>9</sup> [Luis Elizondo](/people/luis-elizondo) kept the investigation going for a few years until his frustration with a lack of interest from DoD leadership became too much to bear.<sup>10</sup> Separately, defense officials declined to release the location and date of an incident showing a [United States Navy (USN)](/organisations/united-states-navy-usn) F/A-18 Super Hornet encounter with an object surrounded by a glowing aura.<sup>11</sup>

In leaving DoD, Elizondo joined [Harold E. Puthoff](/people/harold-e-puthoff) and [Christopher K. Mellon](/people/christopher-k-mellon) - another former DoD official who had served as deputy assistant secretary of defense for intelligence - in the new venture [To the Stars Academy of Arts and Science (TTSA)](/organisations/to-the-stars-academy-of-arts-and-science-ttsa).<sup>12</sup> Elizondo resigned from DoD in protest at excessive secrecy and internal opposition to research on the UAP issue,<sup>13</sup> joining TTSA in October 2017, days after his resignation.<sup>14</sup> Before leaving, he arranged for the Pentagon to release cockpit-camera footage from F/A-18 fighter jets.<sup>15</sup> The first two of these DoD videos were posted by TTSA in mid-December 2017, timed to coincide with New York Times, Politico, and Washington Post reporting on AATIP's existence,<sup>16</sup> and TTSA posted a third video, which it dubbed the [Go-Fast Video](/documents/go-fast-video), in March 2018.<sup>17</sup> At the time it was recorded, no one within DoD or the wider Intelligence Community (IC) could explain the object it showed.<sup>18</sup> The Pentagon later disputed the scope of Elizondo's role: according to The Black Vault, it said he had held no responsibilities regarding AATIP during his time in the [Office of the Under Secretary of Defense for Intelligence (OUSDI)](/organisations/office-of-the-under-secretary-of-defense-for-intelligence-ousdi), up to his resignation effective 2017-10-04.<sup>19</sup> Pentagon spokesman Christopher Sherwood acknowledged that Elizondo had worked for other DoD organisations.<sup>20</sup> Mellon, who had joined the Secretary of Defense's staff under William Cohen,<sup>21</sup> later worked with Elizondo to try to elevate the UAP issue within DoD to the Secretary of Defense.<sup>22</sup>

(UAP)](/topics/unidentified-aerial-phenomena-unidentified-aerial-phenomena-unidentified-anomalous-phenomena-uap).<sup>25</sup> DoD said the release was intended to clear up public misconceptions about A 2023 evaluation by the U.S. Department of Defense Inspector General found that DoD had not used a coordinated approach to detect, report, collect, analyse, and identify UAP.<sup>33</sup> The Inspector General reported that DoD components had largely excluded geographic combatant commands from developing UAP policies and procedures,<sup>34</sup> and had developed varying processes to collect, analyse, and identify UAP incidents,<sup>35</sup> resulting in a response that was uncoordinated and concentrated within each military department.<sup>36</sup> It found that DoD had not issued a comprehensive UAP response plan identifying roles, responsibilities, and coordination procedures,<sup>37</sup> and, lacking an overarching policy, could not be assured that national security and flight-safety threats from UAP had been identified and mitigated.<sup>38</sup> The Inspector General recommended that the Under Secretary of Defense for Intelligence and Security, in coordination with the AARO Director, issue a department-wide UAP policy.<sup>39</sup>
