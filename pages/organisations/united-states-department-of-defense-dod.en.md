The United States Department of Defense (DoD) is the primary federal body responsible for US military operations and national security. Its engagement with [Unidentified Anomalous Phenomena (UAP)](/topics/unidentified-anomalous-phenomena-uap) spans more than seven decades, characterised by periods of active investigation interspersed with long gaps and, according to its own Inspector General, a persistent absence of coordinated policy.<sup>1</sup> The DoD has relied heavily on the military services and their components to detect, report, collect, analyse, and identify UAP since the 1940s.<sup>2</sup> The department's first official UAP-focused activity occurred in December 1947, when the US Air Force Chief of Staff established Project Sign to investigate the first recorded observations of [Unidentified Flying Objects (UFOs)](/topics/unidentified-flying-object-ufo).<sup>1</sup> Project Sign staff drafted a report assessing that at least some UFOs were of interplanetary origin, but DoD leadership rejected it on the basis that it lacked proof, and it was never published.<sup>3</sup> The US government publicly ignored UFOs after closing Project Blue Book in 1969,<sup>4</sup> and the DoD did not officially revisit the subject until the early 2000s, when select members of [United States Congress](/organisations/united-states-congress) initiated and funded the [Advanced Aerospace Weapon Systems Applications Program (AAWSAP)](/projects/advanced-aerospace-weapon-systems-applications-program-advanced-aerospace-weapon-system-applications-program-advanced-aerospace-weapon-systems-applications-program-aawsap) under the direction of the [Defense Intelligence Agency (DIA)](/organisations/defense-intelligence-agency-dia).<sup>5</sup> Over that entire span, DoD efforts to identify and understand UAP have been described as infrequent and inconclusive, driven by competing priorities and a lack of substantive progress.<sup>6</sup>

The AAWSAP contract, funded at approximately 22 million US dollars — an amount described as almost impossible to locate within the broader DoD budget — was the department's principal UAP research vehicle of the 2000s.<sup>7</sup> The names AAWSAP and [Advanced Aerospace Threat Identification Program (AATIP)](/organisations/advanced-aerospace-threat-identification-program-aatip) were used interchangeably on official documentation, but AATIP was never an official DoD programme in its own right.<sup>8</sup> AAWSAP/AATIP was terminated in 2012 upon completion of its deliverables, due to DIA and DoD concerns about the project.<sup>9</sup> The DoD, when asked about the programme in 2017, stated it had never previously acknowledged AATIP's existence and that it had been shut down in 2012;<sup>10</sup> however, backers of the programme maintained that officials continued for five years afterwards to investigate UAP episodes as part of their other DoD duties.<sup>11</sup> [Luis Elizondo](/people/luis-elizondo), who has widely claimed to have headed AATIP as a secretive Pentagon programme, submitted a resignation letter to then-Secretary of Defense James Mattis dated 2017-10-04;<sup>12</sup> the Pentagon publicly stated that Elizondo had no assigned responsibilities for AATIP while working within the [Office of the Under Secretary of Defense for Intelligence (OUSDI)](/organisations/office-of-the-under-secretary-of-defense-for-intelligence-ousdi).<sup>13</sup> Elizondo subsequently joined [To the Stars Academy of Arts and Science (TTSA)](/organisations/to-the-stars-academy-of-arts-and-science-ttsa) alongside [Christopher K. Mellon](/people/christopher-k-mellon), a former Deputy Assistant Secretary of Defense for Intelligence who served under two presidents.

The 2017 publication of leaked DoD infrared video footage in the press marked a turning point in the department's public posture.<sup>14</sup> On 2020-04-27, the DoD formally authorised the release of three unclassified US Navy UAP videos — footage that had previously circulated without official sanction — stating that the release was intended to clear up public misconceptions about whether the footage was real and whether additional material existed.<sup>15</sup> The department determined that the authorised release did not reveal any sensitive capabilities or systems.<sup>16</sup> In August 2020, the DoD established the [Unidentified Aerial Phenomena Task Force (UAPTF)](/organisations/unidentified-aerial-phenomena-task-force-uaptf) to develop standardised reporting requirements for UAP encounters.<sup>17</sup> In November 2021, the Deputy Secretary of Defense directed the establishment of the Airborne Object Identification and Management Synchronization Group (AOIMSG) as the UAPTF's successor, with a mandate to synchronise efforts across the DoD and other federal departments to detect, identify, and characterise objects of interest in special use airspace.<sup>18</sup> On 2022-07-20, the DoD established the [All-domain Anomaly Resolution Office (AARO)](/organisations/all-domain-anomaly-resolution-office-aaro) to absorb the duties previously assigned to the AOIMSG and to serve as the formal interagency entity for UAP detection, collection, analysis, and reporting.<sup>19</sup>

The DoD has faced public claims from multiple individuals regarding alleged concealed UAP programmes. [David Charles Grusch](/people/david-charles-grusch), a former National Reconnaissance Office representative to the UAP Task Force, made public allegations that non-human origin craft and materials had been recovered. The DoD denied that AARO had uncovered the sort of activity Grusch alleged,<sup>20</sup> and released a statement that AARO had not discovered any verifiable information to substantiate claims that programmes involving the possession or reverse-engineering of extraterrestrial materials have existed in the past or exist currently.<sup>21</sup> Grusch had received prior approval from the DoD's Prepublication and Security Review office before making his statements publicly. The U.S. Department of Defense Office of Inspector General separately reviewed DoD policies and actions for detecting, reporting, collecting, analysing, and identifying UAP and determined that the DoD has not issued a comprehensive UAP response plan identifying roles, responsibilities, and coordination procedures,<sup>22</sup> and that it has no overarching UAP policy — leaving the department without assurance that national security and flight safety threats from UAP have been identified and mitigated.<sup>23</sup> [David Fravor](/people/david-fravor), the naval aviator who encountered an unidentified object during a 2004 training exercise, stated that the US government has said it does not know what the object was, making it one of the unexplained cases.

The DoD's institutional relationship with the [United States Intelligence Community](/organisations/united-states-intelligence-community) and with bodies such as the [Office of the Director of National Intelligence (ODNI)](/organisations/office-of-the-director-of-national-intelligence-odni) and the [Department of Homeland Security (DHS)](/organisations/department-of-homeland-security-dhs) has been central to subsequent UAP governance. The [National Defense Authorization Act for Fiscal Year 2023](/documents/national-defense-authorization-act-for-fiscal-year-2023) tasked the Secretary of Defense, in consultation with the Director of National Intelligence, with establishing for the first time a secure mechanism for the authorised reporting of sensitive UAP information to defence channels. AARO subsequently sent guidance to DoD elements, Intelligence Community elements, the Department of Energy, and DHS to review and provide any nondisclosure agreements pertaining to UAP. The All-domain Anomaly Resolution Office (AARO) Historical Record Report (HR2) was produced in response to a requirement in the same legislation, directing AARO to submit to congressional defence and intelligence committees a written report detailing the historical record of US government involvement with [Unidentified Anomalous Phenomena](/topics/unidentified-anomalous-phenomena-uap). The the three Department of Defense Unidentified Aerial Phenomena (UAP) videos remain among the most publicly scrutinised outputs of this evolving institutional posture, representing the DoD's first formal acknowledgement that footage of objects its pilots could not identify was genuine. Despite successive organisational reforms, the Inspector General's 2023 evaluation concluded that the DoD had not used a coordinated approach to detect, report, collect, analyse, and identify UAP, and that military services had largely been waiting for central DoD guidance before developing their own.
