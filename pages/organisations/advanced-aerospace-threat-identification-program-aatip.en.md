The Advanced Aerospace Threat Identification Program (AATIP) was a Pentagon programme<sup>1</sup> that pursued research and investigation into Unidentified Anomalous Phenomena (UAP).<sup>2</sup> The New York Times, in reporting that helped bring the programme to public attention, places its start in 2007 and notes that parts of it remain classified.<sup>3</sup> A later historical review by the [All-domain Anomaly Resolution Office (AARO)](/organisations/all-domain-anomaly-resolution-office-aaro) instead traces the effort to 2009, when the [Defense Intelligence Agency (DIA)](/organisations/defense-intelligence-agency-dia) established the [Advanced Aerospace Weapon Systems Applications Program (AAWSAP)](/projects/advanced-aerospace-weapon-systems-applications-program-advanced-aerospace-weapon-system-applications-program-advanced-aerospace-weapon-systems-applications-program-aawsap) in coordination with the [Office of the Under Secretary of Defense for Intelligence (OUSDI)](/organisations/office-of-the-under-secretary-of-defense-for-intelligence-ousdi), a project the review says was also known as AATIP.<sup>4</sup> The same AARO review states that, unlike AAWSAP, AATIP was never an official Department of Defense programme in its own right: once AAWSAP was cancelled, the AATIP name continued to be used by individuals associated with an informal, unofficial [Unidentified Aerial Phenomena](/topics/unidentified-aerial-phenomena-unidentified-aerial-phenomena-unidentified-anomalous-phenomena-uap) community of interest inside the department.<sup>5</sup> The programme was launched from OUSDI and was managed by [Luis Elizondo](/people/luis-elizondo), a career counterintelligence officer.<sup>6</sup>

AATIP was funded largely at the request of [Harry Reid](/people/harry-reid), the Senate majority leader at the time,<sup>7</sup> and most of the money went to an aerospace research company run by [Robert Bigelow](/people/robert-bigelow), a billionaire entrepreneur and longtime friend of Reid's.<sup>8</sup> Bigelow Aerospace Advanced Space Studies won the initial AAWSAP/AATIP contract.<sup>9</sup> Within the [Department of Defense](/organisations/united-states-department-of-defense-dod)'s roughly $600 billion annual budget, the programme's $22 million was almost impossible to find,<sup>10</sup> and the [Pentagon](/organisations/the-pentagon) wanted the funding kept that way.<sup>11</sup> Ted Stevens and Daniel K. Inouye, senior members of a defense spending subcommittee, supported the funding,<sup>12</sup> and none of Reid, Stevens or Inouye wanted a public debate about it on the Senate floor.<sup>13</sup> In June 2009 Reid wrote requesting that the programme be granted Special Access Program status;<sup>14</sup> his request to [William Lynn III](/people/william-lynn-iii), recorded in a 2009 memo, was denied.<sup>15</sup>

Elizondo was asked to join the programme in 2008<sup>16</sup> and by 2010 had taken charge of it, running it from the Office of the Secretary of Defense under OUSDI.<sup>17</sup> Its mission, per Elizondo, was to collect and analyse information involving [anomalous aerial vehicles (AAV)](/topics/anomalous-aerial-vehicle-anomalous-aerial-vehicle-aav).<sup>18</sup> Investigators reported vehicles studied by the programme reaching 600 to 700 G-forces,<sup>19</sup> flying at approximately 21,000 kilometres per hour,<sup>20</sup> evading radar,<sup>21</sup> and moving through air and water, and possibly space,<sup>22</sup> with no obvious signs of propulsion, wings, or control surfaces,<sup>23</sup> and appearing to defy the natural effects of Earth's gravity.<sup>24</sup> The programme was staffed by a loose-knit, often part-time mix of scientists, electro-optical engineers, avionics specialists and intelligence experts,<sup>25</sup> who weighed prosaic explanations - such as Chinese cruise missile technology<sup>26</sup> or a high-altitude reconnaissance balloon<sup>27</sup> - before treating a sighting as unexplained.

Working with Bigelow's company under AAWSAP, the effort produced documents describing sightings of aircraft that seemed to move at very high velocities with no visible signs of propulsion<sup>28</sup> and that hovered with no apparent means of lift.<sup>29</sup> AATIP collected video and audio recordings of reported incidents, including footage from a Navy F/A-18 Super Hornet showing an aircraft surrounded by a glowing aura, travelling at high speed and rotating as it moved.<sup>30</sup> Officials with the programme also studied videos of encounters between unknown objects and American military aircraft.<sup>31</sup> In August 2017 it released a video, filmed in 2004, of a whitish oval object roughly the size of a commercial plane, chased by two Navy F/A-18F fighter jets from the aircraft carrier USS Nimitz off the coast of San Diego.<sup>32</sup>

[Jay Stratton](/people/jay-stratton) and Elizondo personally handpicked everyone involved in the programme.<sup>33</sup> Stratton had investigated the 2004 USS Nimitz encounter before Elizondo joined the team<sup>34</sup> and wrote a detailed report on it.<sup>35</sup> [Harold E. Puthoff](/people/harold-e-puthoff), a contractor and senior advisor to AAWSAP/AATIP, continued attending meetings, providing briefings and gaining access to videos under Elizondo's leadership until Elizondo's resignation.<sup>36</sup> [Eric Davis](/people/eric-davis), an astrophysicist with high-level national security clearances, worked with Puthoff as a contractor on the programme.<sup>37</sup> Elizondo, together with Jim Semivan and John Robert, later moved what remained of AATIP away from the DIA, housing it within his own portfolio;<sup>38</sup> by then the only contractors still involved were Puthoff, William "Will" Livingston, and Davis.<sup>39</sup> Neill Tipton helped build the programme.<sup>40</sup> Stratton later said he changed the phenomenon's official acronym from [UFO](/topics/unidentified-flying-object-ufo) (Unidentified Flying Object) to UAP (Unidentified Aerial Phenomena) while working at Skinwalker Ranch with the AATIP team.<sup>41</sup>

Government funding for AATIP dried up in 2012,<sup>42</sup> and the Advanced Aerospace Weapon System Applications Program/AATIP effort was terminated that year upon completion of its deliverables, amid Defense Intelligence Agency and Department of Defense concerns about the project;<sup>43</sup> the DIA specifically cited a lack of merit and the utility of the deliverables.<sup>44</sup> Elizondo said the only thing that ended was the government funding,<sup>45</sup> after which he continued working with officials from the United States Navy and the Central Intelligence Agency.<sup>46</sup> In 2017, after nine years on the programme and its Department of Defense replacement, Elizondo left the Pentagon, saying his efforts to investigate the phenomena were being obstructed from within.<sup>47</sup> That December, according to The New York Times, Pentagon officials acknowledged the programme's existence in response to the newspaper's questions.<sup>48</sup> Pentagon spokeswoman [Dana White](/people/dana-white) told [Politico](/organisations/politico) that AATIP existed and had been run by Elizondo,<sup>49</sup> though Pentagon spokesperson [Christopher Sherwood](/people/christopher-sherwood) later said he could not confirm White's statement.<sup>50</sup> AARO's 2024 historical review found no substantive UAP case work by AAWSAP/AATIP beyond a review of [Project Blue Book](/projects/project-blue-book) and private cases, interviews of observers, and unrelated work on alleged paranormal activity at a property in Utah.<sup>51</sup> In August 2020 the Pentagon revived the effort under a new name, the UAP Task Force,<sup>52</sup> and Elizondo's public exposure of AATIP's existence was itself a major factor in the eventual creation of that task force and, later, AARO.<sup>53</sup>
