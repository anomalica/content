The United States Air Force has been the primary government body responsible for investigating [Unidentified Flying Object (UFO)](/topics/unidentified-flying-object-ufo) reports in the United States since the late 1940s. In December 1947, the Air Force Chief of Staff established [Project Sign](/projects/project-sign) to investigate the first recorded observations of unidentified flying objects, marking the [United States Department of Defense (DoD)](/organisations/united-states-department-of-defense-dod)'s first official UAP-focused activity.<sup>1</sup> Air Force personnel investigated 243 reported [Unidentified Aerial Phenomena (UAP)](/topics/unidentified-aerial-phenomena-uap) sightings under Project Sign between 1947 and 1949.<sup>2</sup> Following Project Sign, the Air Force continued its investigative mission through [Project GRUDGE](/projects/project-grudge), and after GRUDGE was disestablished, the UFO mission was reorganised in late 1951 into another distinct programme also named Project GRUDGE, led by Captain [Edward Ruppelt](/people/edward-ruppelt).<sup>3</sup>

In 1952, the Air Force Director of Intelligence initiated [Project Blue Book](/projects/project-blue-book) to investigate UFOs,<sup>4</sup> with USAF Director of Intelligence Major General Charles P. Cabell formally establishing the programme to study UFO phenomena.<sup>5</sup> Between 1952 and 1969, Air Force personnel investigated over 12,000 reported sightings under Project Blue Book,<sup>6</sup> with the total recorded at 12,618 UFO sightings across the period from 1947 to 1969.<sup>7</sup> The official conclusions of Project Blue Book held that no UFO reported, investigated, and evaluated demonstrated any indication of a threat to national security,<sup>8</sup> and that there was no evidence of technological developments or principles beyond the range of then-present-day scientific knowledge.<sup>9</sup> The Air Force also funded an 18-month scientific study on flying saucers under a 325,000 US dollar contract to the University of Colorado, directed by physicist Dr. Edward U. Condon.<sup>10</sup> The overall UFO studies concluded that most sightings involved stars, clouds, conventional aircraft, or spy planes, although 701 remained unexplained.<sup>11</sup>

Throughout the period of its UFO investigations, the Air Force's conduct attracted significant criticism regarding its stated objectivity. [Captain Ruppelt](/people/edward-ruppelt), the first chief of Project Blue Book, later wrote that he was expected to explain away every report and that the Air Force sought to produce press stories in alignment with its own position.<sup>12</sup> [J. Allen Hynek](/people/j-allen-hynek), a scientist associated with Project Blue Book, similarly said the Air Force expected him to perform the role of debunker.<sup>13</sup> The Air Force also implemented a public affairs campaign designed to persuade the public that UFOs constituted nothing unusual or extraordinary, with the stated goal of alleviating public anxiety.<sup>14</sup> The early internal assessment known as the Estimate of the Situation, which reportedly concluded that UFOs were of extraterrestrial origin, was rejected by USAF Chief of Staff General [Hoyt S. Vandenberg](/people/hoyt-s-vandenberg) as lacking proof.<sup>15</sup> The [All-Domain Anomaly Resolution Office (AARO)](/organisations/all-domain-anomaly-resolution-office-aaro) subsequently found no evidence to suggest that the Air Force had a policy intended to cover up evidence of extraterrestrial knowledge or material, concluding instead that the Air Force sought to focus on what it determined to be more important concerns, such as Soviet technology and United States defence readiness.<sup>16</sup>

The [Roswell incident (1947)](/events/roswell-incident-1947) became the most high-profile episode associated with Air Force UFO investigations. The Air Force conducted a systematic search of archives and records centres in support of the [Government Accountability Office](/organisations/government-accountability-office)'s audit of the incident.<sup>17</sup> The Air Force published [The Roswell Report: Fact versus Fiction in the New Mexico Desert (1995)](/documents/the-roswell-report-fact-versus-fiction-in-the-new-mexico-desert-1995) in 1995, which stated that its research did not locate or develop any information indicating the Roswell incident was a UFO event, nor any government cover-up.<sup>18</sup> A follow-on report, [The Roswell Report: Case Closed (1997)](/documents/the-roswell-report-case-closed-1997), published in 1997, provided additional materials and analysis supporting the conclusion that debris recovered near Roswell originated from the US Army Air Force's balloon-borne programme, and that reports of recovered flying saucers and crew were descriptions of Air Force personnel engaged in dummy recovery operations.<sup>19</sup>

In the contemporary period, the Air Force has continued to engage with UAP reporting and investigation alongside the [United States Navy](/organisations/united-states-navy) and [United States Army](/organisations/united-states-army). The Air Force designated UAP as a Special Interest Item, requiring aircrews to document and report UAP observations within 24 hours of engine shutdown and mission completion using the [Marauder Mission Reporting System](/objects/marauder-mission-reporting-system),<sup>20</sup> a system also used by [United States Army](/organisations/united-states-army) personnel as the Army has not developed its own UAP-specific processes.<sup>21</sup> The [Evaluation of the DoD's Actions Regarding Unidentified Anomalous Phenomena (DODIG-2023-109)](/documents/evaluation-of-the-dods-actions-regarding-unidentified-anomalous-phenomena-dodig-2023-109) recommended that the Secretary of the Air Force issue interim guidance for UAP while awaiting Department of Defense policy, integrating existing intelligence, counterintelligence, and force protection policies and procedures.<sup>22</sup> The Secretary of the Air Force did not provide official comments for inclusion in that report as requested,<sup>23</sup> and the Air Force's informal response did not provide specific actions or dates, leaving the relevant recommendation resolved but open.<sup>24</sup> Sources cited in reporting on [Unidentified Anomalous Phenomena](/concepts/unidentified-anomalous-phenomena) have noted that the Air Force has been less forthcoming than other agencies with regard to UAP.<sup>25</sup> [David Grusch](/people/david-grusch), a decorated former Air Force officer who served 14 years and reached the rank of Major,<sup>26</sup> became notable for making public claims regarding non-human origin craft, with the [Air Force Office of Special Investigations (AFOSI)](/organisations/air-force-office-of-special-investigations-afosi) among the organisations connected to the broader UAP oversight framework in which he operated. The [Advanced Aerospace Threat Identification Program](/organisations/advanced-aerospace-threat-identification-program) review process also involved the Air Force alongside the [United States Navy](/organisations/united-states-navy) and the Defense Intelligence Agency.<sup>27</sup> The [1997 Phoenix Lights incident](/events/1997-phoenix-lights-incident), one of the most widely witnessed UAP events on record, prompted an Air Force explanation that it was an [A-10 Thunderbolt II](/objects/a-10-thunderbolt-ii) dropping flares, a conclusion disputed by some witnesses and commentators.<sup>28</sup> The [1980 Rendlesham Forest incident](/events/1980-rendlesham-forest-incident) in England similarly involved US servicemen stationed at an Air Force installation.<sup>29</sup>
