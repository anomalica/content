At midday on 1947-07-08, Colonel William Blanchard ordered his base public information officer, Lieutenant Walter Haut, to issue a press release announcing that the Army had recovered a flying disc, part of what became known as the [Roswell incident (1947)](/events/roswell-incident-1947).<sup>1</sup> That initial release referred specifically to a recovered 'flying disc';<sup>2</sup> the Army never in fact used the term 'flying saucer', referring to the object only as a 'disc'.<sup>3</sup> According to In Plain Sight, the Army was forced to admit that same year that it had no idea what the flying discs were.<sup>4</sup> The same account records that secret Army memos declassified in 1994 likewise referred to the object as a 'flying disc'.<sup>5</sup> Of the 1947 Roswell Unidentified Aerial Phenomena (UAP) crash, government investigators initially assumed the craft was from another nation, possibly on a reconnaissance mission, but the Army determined within hours that it had not been made by humans.<sup>6</sup> In Plain Sight also records an allegation that a separate craft, said to have crashed at Aztec, New Mexico, in 1948, was retrieved by the military under a cloak of secrecy.<sup>7</sup>

The Army's presence in the region predates Roswell: it conducted the world's first-ever detonation of the Trinity nuclear device at White Sands, New Mexico, on 1945-07-16.<sup>8</sup> In 1958 the Army and the [United States Air Force (USAF)](/organisations/united-states-air-force-usaf) jointly took over the Avrocar project from Avro, renaming it 'Project Silver Bug'.<sup>9</sup>

In the 1970s, the [Central Intelligence Agency (CIA)](/organisations/central-intelligence-agency-cia), the Army and the [Defense Intelligence Agency (DIA)](/organisations/defense-intelligence-agency-dia) recruited individuals said to possess [extrasensory perception (ESP)](/topics/extrasensory-perception-esp) to help uncover intelligence secrets.<sup>10</sup> After the CIA's own ESP work concluded in the late 1970s, the programme moved to the Army's base at Fort Meade, Maryland,<sup>11</sup> where the Army formally decided to find, train and use [remote viewers](/topics/remote-viewing-rv) under [Project STARGATE](/projects/project-stargate),<sup>12</sup> a classified programme run in partnership with SRI International and also referred to as [The Stargate Project](/projects/the-stargate-project).<sup>13</sup> Recruiters compiled a list of indicators to help identify candidates, including consistent success regardless of the task<sup>14</sup> and being well liked by peers while nonetheless regarded as different from the norm.<sup>15</sup> [Joseph McMoneagle](/people/joseph-mcmoneagle), who came to remote viewing from a background in Army Intelligence technical collection systems<sup>16</sup> and retired from the Army holding the rank of CW2 (Chief Warrant Officer 2),<sup>17</sup> was one of the programme's original remote viewers<sup>18</sup> and helped the Army locate hostages in Iran.<sup>19</sup> [Ed Dames](/people/ed-dames), a retired Army major,<sup>20</sup> was one of five prototype Army members selected to be trained by [Ingo Swann](/people/ingo-swann) during 1983 and 1984,<sup>21</sup> after [Harold E. Puthoff](/people/harold-e-puthoff) told Swann that the Army was funding the training.<sup>22</sup> Lyn Buchanan, also a retired Army remote viewer, was part of the Stargate Project,<sup>23</sup> as was Paul H. Smith, a former Army major and one of the programme's principals.<sup>24</sup> A later neurological study of UAP experiencers by Garry P. Nolan found that some of the Army's former psychic spies from the remote-viewing programme shared the same hyperdeveloped caudate-putamen identified in experiencers.<sup>25</sup> Craig McPherson, another Army veteran linked to UAP-related claims, served in the Army Infantry in Europe from June 1944 until October 1946<sup>26</sup> and held the rank of sergeant by his discharge.<sup>27</sup>

In October 2019 it was announced that the [To the Stars Academy of Arts and Science (TTSA)](/organisations/to-the-stars-academy-of-arts-and-science-ttsa) was partnering with the Army to research alleged metamaterials that TTSA's sources claimed to have obtained from crashed craft.<sup>28</sup> Samples known as Art's Parts were sent to an Army laboratory to test whether they originated from an alien spacecraft,<sup>29</sup> under an agreement secured through a Cooperative Research and Development Agreement (CRADA) between TTSA and the Army,<sup>30</sup> which allowed the Army to make its historical data, laboratories and scientists available for the testing.<sup>31</sup> According to In Plain Sight, an Army public relations officer said the materials research was of key interest to Army research;<sup>32</sup> under the agreement, the Army supplied expertise and facilities to analyse the samples while TTSA supplied the materials themselves, with no financial compensation changing hands in either direction,<sup>33</sup> and any invention resulting from a major breakthrough would be jointly owned.<sup>34</sup> The same account records that researcher John Greenewald reported via The Black Vault, on 2019-10-18, that the Army had released its agreement with TTSA.<sup>35</sup> The Army went on to conduct its own in-house analysis of the material; the [All-domain Anomaly Resolution Office (AARO)](/organisations/all-domain-anomaly-resolution-office-aaro) broadly agreed with the Army's conclusions but found the structure was not purely a layered magnesium alloy and bismuth.<sup>36</sup> AARO ultimately concluded that a sample from an alleged crashed off-world spacecraft, acquired from a private UAP investigating organisation and from the Army, was a manufactured, terrestrial alloy that did not represent off-world technology or possess any exceptional qualities.<sup>37</sup>

[Luis Elizondo](/people/luis-elizondo) joined the Army after college,<sup>38</sup> beginning his career at an Army recruiter's office in Miami, Florida, in 1995<sup>39</sup> and completing basic training that same year.<sup>40</sup> He enlisted as an Army grunt rather than accept an officer's commission, in order to avoid being assigned to the medical corps against his preference,<sup>41</sup> and trained as an Army counterintelligence special agent, an occupational specialty known as 97-Bravo.<sup>42</sup> He was recommended by Lieutenant Colonel Michael Seage for a position in the Army's Great Skills program,<sup>43</sup> a long-running Army intelligence programme that recruited young soldiers identified as having special talents and trained and employed them as soldier spies.<sup>44</sup> Eugene Lessman was the lead recruiter for classified activities within the Army<sup>45</sup> and a Military Intelligence Civilian Accepted Career Program (MICACP) civilian case officer.<sup>46</sup> Elizondo's career would later briefly cross that of Puthoff, whom he first met in person years afterwards at a programme dinner in Roslyn, Virginia.<sup>47</sup>
