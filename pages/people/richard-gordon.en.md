Richard Gordon was a Doctor of Medicine specialising in internal medicine.<sup>1</sup> He first met [Robert Monroe](/people/robert-monroe) in 1942 in [New York City New York](/places/new-york-new-york-city-usa)<sup>2</sup> and became Monroe's family doctor.<sup>3</sup> Gordon was in his fifties when the two first met.<sup>4</sup> According to Monroe, Mrs. Gordon told him that at twenty-two Gordon had been very active and eager, always in a hurry, with a big shock of blond hair.<sup>5</sup> Monroe said he considered [Agnew Bahnson](/people/agnew-bahnson) similarly too interested in new things and experiences to dwell in the past, comparing him in this respect to Gordon.<sup>6</sup>

In 1958, Monroe consulted Gordon, his longtime family physician and diagnostician, about a series of vibration episodes he had been experiencing.<sup>7</sup> According to Monroe, after a thorough physical examination Gordon found nothing physically wrong, suggested he had been working too hard, and recommended more sleep and losing weight, dismissing the possibility of a brain tumour or epilepsy.<sup>8</sup> The following week, Gordon carried out further tests - blood tests, fluoroscopes, electrocardiograms and a urinalysis - checking carefully for indications of brain lesions.<sup>9</sup> According to Monroe, Gordon also arranged an [electroencephalogram (EEG)](/topics/electroencephalography-electroencephalogram-eeg) brain-wave analysis, which showed no unusual problem.<sup>10</sup> Monroe said Gordon prescribed tranquilizers and advised him to lose weight, smoke less and rest more, telling him that if he had a problem it was not a physical one.<sup>11</sup>

In the spring of 1961, Monroe visited Gordon at his office and had lunch with him there.<sup>12</sup> About a week later, Gordon and his wife sailed to Spain for a European trip.<sup>13</sup> According to Monroe, Mrs. Gordon phoned him six weeks later to say Gordon had fallen sick in Europe and the couple had cut short their trip.<sup>14</sup> Monroe said Gordon refused treatment overseas, insisted on returning home, and went immediately into hospital for an exploratory operation<sup>15</sup>, which, according to Monroe, found an abdominal cancer that was beyond treatment.<sup>16</sup> Monroe wrote to Gordon describing his own [out-of-body experiences](/topics/out-of-body-experience-obe) and a technique for leaving the physical body; at Gordon's own request, Mrs. Gordon read the letter to him repeatedly in hospital.<sup>17</sup> Several weeks later, Gordon fell into a coma and died peacefully without regaining consciousness.<sup>18</sup>

Gordon was the first person close to Monroe to die since Monroe had developed his own out-of-body ability.<sup>19</sup> Some months later, on a Saturday afternoon, Monroe attempted an out-of-body journey to find him, taking about an hour to reach the vibrational state before leaving his body.<sup>20</sup> That first attempt lasted two hours away from his physical body and ended in failure to find Gordon.<sup>21</sup> During one such attempt, Monroe became increasingly and unbearably hot while waiting to see Gordon, and eventually had to leave without seeing him.<sup>22</sup> On a second attempt the following Saturday, a voice asked Monroe why he wanted to see Gordon again, since he had already seen him the previous Saturday.<sup>23</sup> Monroe concluded that a short, thin young man of about twenty-two with a big shock of hair, whom he had seen during his first visit, was Gordon as he would have appeared at twenty-two rather than at seventy.<sup>24</sup> At a later visit to the home of Gordon's widow, Monroe saw an old photograph of Gordon at twenty-two that matched perfectly the young man he had seen.<sup>25</sup> Monroe never saw the helper who took him on an out-of-body voyage to visit Gordon in Locale II, but felt the helper's hands and heard his voice.<sup>26</sup>

Separately, NASA mission audio associates a Richard Gordon with the Apollo 12 mission: aboard Yankee Clipper during a rendezvous procedure, he was unable to find Intrepid's tracking light in his sextant because the light appeared to have burned out.<sup>27</sup> [Charlie Hickson](/people/charlie-hickson) said that Gordon had set up the television camera and told Charles "Pete" Conrad it was ready.<sup>28</sup> In a later Apollo 17 crew medical debriefing, Harrison Schmitt recalled that Dick Gordon had said that once you start flying, the clock is relentless.<sup>29</sup>
