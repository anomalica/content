Diana Walsh Pasulka is a professor of religious studies<sup>1</sup> who began researching the [Unidentified Flying Object (UFO)](/topics/unidentified-flying-object-ufo) phenomenon in January 2012<sup>2</sup>. Raised as a born-again Christian from the age of eleven, she attends a Catholic church<sup>3</sup>. Her fieldwork among ufologists, scientists and religious experiencers is recorded in her book American Cosmic (2019), in the course of which she coined the term "meta-experiencer" for scientists who drew on the accounts of primary UFO experiencers while remaining reluctant to admit they believed the phenomenon was real<sup>4</sup>.

Central to her account is Tyler D., an employee in the space industry<sup>5</sup> whom Pasulka describes as a wealthy rocket scientist<sup>6</sup> and both an aeronautical engineer and a biomedical entrepreneur<sup>7</sup>. Tyler D. was not invited to a small closed conference Pasulka organised, bringing together ufologists and scientists with scholars of the humanities who studied the UFO phenomenon<sup>8</sup>; he telephoned her the day after the conference to invite her to a site in [New Mexico USA](/places/new-mexico-usa) to learn about the physical nature of the phenomenon<sup>9</sup>. Pasulka travelled there with Tyler D. and James Master; the three wore blindfolds for the final forty minutes of the drive to the site<sup>10</sup>, and Tyler D. drove them for forty minutes over a gravel road to reach it<sup>11</sup>.

At the site, the group used a metal detector to locate a small metallic object, subsequently identified as the artifact<sup>12</sup>; James Master found it lodged in a crevice between rocks in a gully<sup>13</sup>. Tyler D. photographed the specimen and the location where it was found, labelled it with the date, and placed it in a specimen bag<sup>14</sup>, and James Master agreed to take the recovered artifacts with him to study them<sup>15</sup>. At the airport afterwards, James Master's carry-on bag caused an X-ray scanner to shut down<sup>16</sup>, and security searched him, his suitcase and his jacket<sup>17</sup>. James Master, one of the world's leading scientists<sup>18</sup>, holds an endowed chair in molecular biology and heads a laboratory at one of the world's top universities<sup>19</sup>.

Pasulka's research also involved [Jacques Vallée](/people/jacques-vallee), described as a researcher, astronomer and computer scientist<sup>20</sup>. According to Pasulka, Vallée's approach to the UFO phenomenon addressed three aspects: the physical, the testimonial evidence of experiencers, and the social persistence of belief in the phenomenon regardless of verifiable evidence<sup>21</sup>. Vallée once told her emphatically to trust no one, and not even to trust what she saw<sup>22</sup>; together the two interviewed a witness who had seen and photographed a starlike object near Santa Cruz<sup>23</sup>. Pasulka has written that Donna Haraway once asked her to consider what was happening in a person's mind when looking at images on film, or in the minds of those who witnessed apparitions of the Virgin Mary<sup>24</sup>; she had earlier interviewed Haraway during her office hours to explain a plan to study apparitions of the Virgin Mary in Medjugorje<sup>25</sup>.

Pasulka joined In the Field, a study group of UFO photographers and videographers created and moderated by Scott Browne<sup>26</sup>, after telling Browne she studied the UFO phenomenon and worked with Vallée<sup>27</sup>. Browne's work within [ufology](/topics/ufology) has drawn hostility: he has been called a "debunker" and worse across social media<sup>28</sup>, and has received direct threats of bodily and emotional harm<sup>29</sup>. Pasulka has written that Browne exposed a set of viral images purporting to show UFOs by enlarging and comparing them side by side, showing they were in fact differently shaped balloons<sup>30</sup>.

Pasulka's research also took her to [Rome](/places/rome-italy), where the door of the church of Santa Sabina carries images of levitation, including a depiction of the ascension of Christ across two panels followed by a third showing him ascended<sup>31</sup>. During a golf-cart tour of Rome with Tyler D., the pair were stopped by police for around twenty minutes before reaching the church<sup>32</sup>. On another occasion, after nearly two hours of negotiation by his translator, Tyler D. obtained Pasulka's agreement that he could reveal his identity to Vatican Secret Archives security<sup>33</sup>. Elsewhere in her account, Pasulka traces a history of rocketry entangled with esoteric belief: Konstantin Tsiolkovsky is regarded as the founding father of rocketry and aeronautics<sup>34</sup> and discovered the equations that later helped scientists develop rockets to take humans off Earth<sup>35</sup>, while [Jack Parsons](/people/jack-parsons) collaborated with Aleister Crowley and L. Ron Hubbard while launching rockets and conducting rituals in the Los Angeles desert<sup>36</sup>, and died at thirty-seven when one of his rockets exploded in his garage<sup>37</sup>. Pasulka owned a pink iPhone<sup>38</sup>, and wrote that watching the monolith in 2001: A Space Odyssey, it reminded her of a larger version of her own phone<sup>39</sup>. She has also described a personal experience of synchronicity: unable to sleep on a New Year's Eve, she opened Friedrich Nietzsche's The Gay Science at random<sup>40</sup>, finding it fell open to one of the book's only three aphorisms devoted to New Year's Eve<sup>41</sup>. Elsewhere, she writes that cultural narratives about the UFO phenomenon form through processes she connects to [Carl Jung](/people/carl-jung)'s concept of amplificatory interpretation<sup>42</sup>.
