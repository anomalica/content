Oliver Harry Turner was an Australian nuclear physicist and Defence Department scientist who, across two decades, produced some of the most detailed classified assessments of unidentified aerial phenomena undertaken within the Australian intelligence establishment. He is known to researchers largely through the work of [Bill Chalker](/people/bill-chalker), who visited him at his home in the summer of 2001<sup>1</sup>. Turner had earlier served as chief health physics officer for the Maralinga nuclear tests<sup>2</sup>, the British atomic bomb trials conducted during the 1950s and early 1960s<sup>2</sup>.

In 1954, while a Melbourne University nuclear physics professor holding an existing security clearance, Turner approached the [Royal Australian Air Force](/organisations/royal-australian-air-force) asking to read its UFO files and was hired by the Directorate of Air Force Intelligence Australia to conduct a classified review of recent sightings<sup>3</sup>, an episode now catalogued as the 1954 Harry Turner Directorate of Air Force Intelligence (DAFI) Unidentified Aerial Phenomena (UAP) review. His resulting report concluded that the evidence presented by the air force tended to support the view that certain strange aircraft had been observed behaving in a manner suggestive of extra-terrestrial origin<sup>4</sup>, and he was rebuffed for these findings by the air force's intelligence branch<sup>5</sup>. Because Turner had quoted the American flying-saucer author [Donald Keyhoe](/people/donald-keyhoe) in his research, Australia's Director of Air Force Intelligence dismissed his findings after consulting with the [United States Air Force (USAF)](/organisations/united-states-air-force-usaf)<sup>6</sup>, on the stated grounds that Turner had erred in citing Keyhoe, whose books supposedly gave a misleading impression of official US government sanction<sup>7</sup>. The Royal Australian Air Force ultimately elected to heed its American ally and play down Turner's extra-terrestrial hypothesis<sup>8</sup>. Now-declassified files show that the same Turner subsequently took part in the British nuclear tests at Maralinga South Australia from 1956 to 1963 as a health physics officer<sup>9</sup>, during which he investigated strange sightings of anomalous objects over the site<sup>10</sup>. His interest in the subject had also been sharpened by the mysterious sightings at Maralinga in 1960<sup>11</sup>: in one instance, a light seen in July 1960 at Wewak Maralinga, Turner concluded it was not a natural phenomenon but a UFO, either a satellite re-entry cone or a flying saucer<sup>12</sup>, while the Range's security officer instead speculated it was a meteor or static electricity<sup>13</sup>.

By the early 1970s Turner was a scientific analyst, and later head of the nuclear science section, within the Australia, Joint Intelligence Bureau, Directorate of Scientific and Technical Intelligence (DSTI)<sup>14, 15</sup>. By 1971 he was firmly convinced the [Unidentified Anomalous Phenomena (UAP)](/topics/unidentified-anomalous-phenomena-uap) phenomenon was real and required urgent investigation<sup>16</sup>, and privately pushed the case at the highest levels of Australia's Defence Department while sightings were being played down in the media and by the air force<sup>17</sup>. This culminated in his declassified 1971 Harry Turner report 'Scientific and Intelligence Aspects of the Unidentified Flying Object (UFO) Problem', which, according to the report, asserted that unidentified objects were real<sup>18</sup> and that there was an ongoing deliberate official US policy to ridicule UFO sightings in order to stifle public awareness of the phenomenon<sup>19</sup>. Turner considered that this facade of ridicule was intended to allay public alarm, deny the Soviet Union a propaganda opening, and conceal a genuine US programme developing vehicles that emulated UFO performance<sup>20</sup>. He asserted that in 1953 the [Air Force Office of Special Investigations (AFOSI)](/organisations/air-force-office-of-special-investigations-afosi) had persuaded the USAF to use the [Project Blue Book](/projects/project-blue-book) investigation as a means of publicly debunking UFOs<sup>21</sup>, while secretly launching a crash programme into [Anti-Gravity Propulsion](/topics/anti-gravity-propulsion)<sup>22</sup>. His paper also criticised the [Condon Report](/documents/condon-report), the US Air Force-funded 1968 study directed by physicist Edward Condon<sup>23</sup>, arguing that its conclusions conflicted with its own contents and had been discredited by many reputable scientists<sup>24</sup>, and he dismissed the official Project Blue Book investigation after its closure by the USAF in 1969<sup>25</sup>. Turner said that many intelligence officers associated with the UFO problem, including former CIA director Roscoe Hillenkoetter, had stated that the US government knew UFOs were extra-terrestrial but was withholding this fact from the public<sup>26</sup>. He met the Project Blue Book scientist [J. Allen Hynek](/people/j-allen-hynek) during Hynek's visit to Australia<sup>27</sup>, and told his superiors that Hynek, along with many other reputable scientists, did not accept the USAF's standard explanations of misidentification, hysteria or hoax<sup>28</sup>.

Turner obtained access to the Australian Air Force's UAP sightings reports<sup>29</sup> and proposed an Australian "rapid intervention team" to investigate UFO events, for which an aircraft was to be kept on standby<sup>30</sup>, a plan that had the approval of Australia's Chief Defence Scientist<sup>31</sup>. His concern was taken very seriously, possibly because sightings of strange objects at Woomera Rocket Range South Australia were still occurring<sup>32</sup>. However, there was bad blood between Turner and the Directorate of Air Force Intelligence Australia because he had criticised its past handling of UAP sightings reports<sup>33</sup>, and the air force subsequently withdrew his access to its sightings files<sup>34</sup>, spiking his rapid intervention team proposal<sup>17</sup>. Bill Chalker later assessed that Turner had in fact been arguing for the air force to be given more resources to investigate UFOs scientifically, but that the Directorate took it as criticism and shut him out<sup>35</sup>. During the late 1960s and early 1970s, Turner fought what is described as a secret battle to encourage a more scientific examination of the UFO mystery within the intelligence and military establishment<sup>36</sup>.

Chalker, who befriended Turner late in his life<sup>37</sup>, considers that political myopia from both the US and Australian military effectively ended Australia's first serious attempt at scientific investigation of UFOs<sup>38</sup>. During his 2001 visit, Turner gave Chalker Harry Turner's ten pages of handwritten notes on Mrs. Klein, covering his 1971-1972 investigation of a Canberra woman he called Mrs. Klein, later identified as Vicki Klein<sup>39</sup> — an episode Anomalica records as the 1971-72 Vicki Klein alien contact and abduction investigation. The mid-1971 rash of Canberra-area UFO sightings had prompted Turner to contact the local Canberra civilian UFO society (formed 1967)<sup>40</sup>, after which Klein made private contact with him, and during a series of meetings at her home between July 1971 and February 1972 described her unfolding alien contact experience<sup>41</sup>. Turner told Chalker that while Klein informed the Canberra group of her sightings, she was careful not to mention the alien dynamic unfolding in her own house<sup>42</sup>. Turner's notes did not record the date of Klein's fourth UFO sighting<sup>43</sup>, so he corresponded with fellow scientist Dr. Michael Duggin, who shared his deep interest in the UFO phenomenon, to supplement the details<sup>44</sup>; Chalker described the two men as part of a secret "invisible college" of scientists researching the mystery<sup>45</sup>. Turner said he was not sure what to make of Klein's information at the time and was simply trying to make an accurate record of her claims<sup>46</sup>. Those claims included Klein's account that the signal for "the beginning of the end" would be the disappearance of a small island followed by a larger one, which she suspected would be in the New Guinea area, with the ensuing chaos lasting three days and worsening before the world was cleansed<sup>47</sup>. According to Chalker, Ron Summers separately confirmed to him the basic story Klein had told Turner<sup>48</sup>, and Chalker found what followed in Turner's notes striking for how closely it resembled the alien-abduction accounts that would come to dominate UFO controversy fifteen years later<sup>49</sup>, prefiguring elements that would not become public until [Budd Hopkins](/people/budd-hopkins) described them in his 1987 book *Intruders*<sup>50</sup>. This encounter with Chalker is separately recorded as Bill Chalker's 2001-07 visit to Oliver Harry Turner.
