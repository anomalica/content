John Mack was a Harvard psychiatrist and researcher<sup>1</sup> who studied people who reported experiences with anomalous phenomena.<sup>1</sup> According to David Charles Grusch, Mack concluded that something real was occurring in the experiences reported to him, and that it was not a psychosomatic or mental health condition.<sup>2</sup> According to American Cosmic, Mack claimed that the experiencers he studied were psychologically healthy and that the experiences they described were common,<sup>3</sup> and he came to see alien abduction experiences as a significant cultural development demanding serious scholarly attention.<sup>4</sup> In the course of his research he approached the philosopher of science Thomas Kuhn, who argued that scientific revolutions came about through attention to anomalies;<sup>5</sup> according to Diana Walsh Pasulka, Kuhn advised Mack to focus on raw data and to persist in collecting it even when it did not fit conventional frameworks of knowledge.<sup>6</sup>

Mack's work is grouped alongside that of [Budd Hopkins](/people/budd-hopkins) as providing circumstantial evidence for alien abduction, including landing-site traces, biopsy scars, alleged implants, and testimony from abducted individuals.<sup>7</sup> By 1991, after a wave of publications on alien abduction from Mack, Hopkins and others, the practice of hypnotic regression used in this research came under scrutiny.<sup>8</sup> In 1994, following the revelation that the Dean of Harvard Medical School had appointed a committee of peers to confidentially review Mack's clinical care and investigation of alleged alien abduction experiencers, Daniel Sheehan defended him;<sup>9</sup> this 1994 Harvard review of John E. Mack put his tenure as a [Harvard University](/organisations/harvard-university) professor under threat.<sup>10</sup> Sheehan's support for Mack during the review was funded by the billionaire Laurance Rockefeller.<sup>11</sup> After a 14-month investigation, Harvard declared Mack's freedom to study what he wished and to state his opinions without impediment.<sup>12</sup> Tom DeLonge said that Mack almost lost his job because he started writing books about UFOs and people getting abducted.<sup>13</sup> Ralph Blumenthal later worked on a biography of Mack, describing him as a Harvard psychiatrist who treated and extensively interviewed people claiming to have been abducted by or to have encountered aliens.<sup>14</sup>

Mack's 1994 book Abduction: Human Encounters with Aliens reached a wide readership; James Master picked it up believing it to be science fiction and found it read like his own biography, which helped him frame his own experiences as UFO-related.<sup>15</sup> Edward Carlos, another experiencer, felt that while Mack was attentive to his feelings, Mack never had a grasp of the real phenomenon.<sup>16</sup>

Mack conducted hypnotic regression sessions with [Peter Khoury](/people/peter-khoury) as part of the Peter Khoury John Mack hypnotic regression sessions. During a 1996 visit to [Australia, New South Wales, Sydney](/places/new-south-wales-sydney-australia), Mack regressed Khoury under hypnosis in an attempt to clarify Khoury's 1988 experience beyond a moment of blankness that had followed the sensation of a needle being inserted in his head, in what became John Mack's 1996 hypnotic regression of Peter Khoury.<sup>17</sup> During the sessions Mack observed a difference in Khoury's demeanour when recalling three beings that looked scary or evil-looking, whom Khoury perceived as robust like military figures, compared with others he perceived as scientists.<sup>18</sup> A female psychiatrist was also present observing one of the sessions.<sup>19</sup> Khoury was sweating heavily while recalling the encounter, then abruptly stopped sweating and became calm the moment he described looking into the eyes of a gold-coloured being;<sup>20</sup> according to Khoury, Mack and the observing psychiatrist agreed that this sudden physiological shift could not have been faked.<sup>21</sup> Khoury also underwent hypnotic regression with Frank Wilkes, one of the first hypnotherapists in the Peter Khoury alien abduction support group.<sup>22</sup> Khoury recounted that Mack later told him, after a workshop while driving home, that he admired and respected how Khoury dealt with his own experience while also helping others deal with theirs;<sup>23</sup> Khoury believes Mack was drawn to him because, unlike other witnesses, he did not push his own story onto Mack.<sup>24</sup>

Mack's research also took him to Zimbabwe, where he investigated the Ariel School incident, and brought him into contact with Credo Mutwa, the Zulu sangoma whose alien-themed paintings, connected to Mantindane (Zulu alien-being lore), predated the recording of his stories by Mack and Stephen Larsen in Credo Mutwa's 1983 alien paintings.<sup>13</sup> Mack reported a rapid healing of a hand injury that he said had followed a 1976 encounter between the Mantindane and Mutwa.<sup>25</sup> Mack's professional difficulties were later compared to those of [Roger Leir](/people/roger-leir), whose licence to practise medicine was likewise challenged and who faced a nuisance lawsuit that harmed his practice.<sup>26</sup>
