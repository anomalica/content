Ryan Graves is a former [United States Navy (USN)](/organisations/united-states-navy-usn) F/A-18 pilot who served for a decade, including two deployments in Operation Enduring Freedom and Operation Inherent Resolve.<sup>1</sup> In 2014 he flew as an F/A-18F Super Hornet pilot with Navy Fighter Attack Squadron 11 (Strike Fighter Squadron 11 (VFA-11)), known as the Red Rippers, stationed at Naval Air Station Oceana in Virginia Beach, Virginia.<sup>2</sup> He was trained as an aviation safety officer by the Navy.<sup>3</sup> He founded [Americans for Safe Aerospace](/organisations/americans-for-safe-aerospace),<sup>4</sup> an organisation advocating for aerospace safety and national security, and is its Executive Director.<sup>5</sup> According to the hearing record, he testified at the House Hearing on Unidentified Anomalous Phenomena (2023-07-26) in that capacity.<sup>6</sup> He is described as a naval pilot and a key eyewitness in the 2015 USS Theodore Roosevelt UAP incursions.<sup>7</sup>

Graves' account centres on the [2014-2015 US East Coast Unidentified Aerial Phenomena (UAP) encounters](/events/2014-2015-us-east-coast-unidentified-aerial-phenomena-uap-encounters). In 2014 his squadron's jets received a radar upgrade that made it possible to use infrared targeting cameras to zero in on [Unidentified Aerial Phenomena (Unidentified Aerial Phenomena / Unidentified Anomalous Phenomena (UAP))](/topics/unidentified-aerial-phenomena-unidentified-aerial-phenomena-unidentified-anomalous-phenomena-uap).<sup>8</sup> After the upgrade, the squadron began detecting unknown objects operating in its airspace,<sup>9</sup> which were hovering over restricted airspace southeast of Virginia Beach.<sup>10</sup> Graves' jet's AN/APG-79 Radar tracked the unidentified objects kilometres away on numerous occasions,<sup>11</sup> and when his jet's [Advanced Targeting Forward-Looking Infrared (ATFLIR) targeting pod](/objects/advanced-targeting-forward-looking-infrared-atflir-targeting-pod) got close enough, it displayed the objects either electro-optically or in infrared.<sup>12</sup> 'We're getting them on radar and then picking them up on the FLIR,' Graves said.<sup>13</sup> What appeared on the cockpit display was not the distinct outline of a normal aircraft, of the kind where one can almost see the rivets.<sup>14</sup> A targeting-camera video of one such object was captured off Jacksonville, Florida, in 2015 by members of Graves' squadron;<sup>15</sup> it showed no exhaust plume.<sup>16</sup> Pilots training off the Atlantic coast encountered unidentified objects every day.<sup>17</sup> On one occasion the objects were already visible when a flight took off, remained throughout a flight of roughly ninety minutes to two hours, and were still there when a fellow pilot took off an hour later.<sup>18</sup> Similar radar detections by aviators with upgraded systems continued along the Eastern Seaboard between 2014 and 2015,<sup>19</sup> with colleagues correlating the radar returns against other onboard sensors and their own sightings.<sup>20</sup> Information about the objects was included in the notice to airmen to prevent accidents.<sup>21</sup>

The observed UAP accelerated to speeds up to Mach 1, the speed of sound.<sup>22</sup> One remained completely stationary at zero airspeed<sup>23</sup> despite Category 4 hurricane-force winds of approximately 220 kilometres per hour.<sup>24</sup> The same object accelerated from zero airspeed to supersonic speeds of approximately 1,360 to 1,480 kilometres per hour (Mach 1.1-1.2) in erratic, quick manoeuvres.<sup>25</sup> A separate, spherical and non-aerodynamically shaped UAP travelled at speeds up to approximately 2,450 kilometres per hour (Mach 2).<sup>26</sup> The objects had no visible means of lift, control surfaces or propulsion - nothing resembling the wings, flaps or engines of a normal aircraft<sup>27</sup> - and outlasted the fighter jets, operating continuously throughout the day.<sup>28</sup> There was not enough detail to determine whether they had visible features such as seams, rivets or sections.<sup>29</sup> Graves, a formally trained engineer, said the technology involved defied his understanding,<sup>30</sup> and that he did not believe a human could survive the g-forces the objects exhibited using technology known today.<sup>31</sup>

During a training mission in Warning Area W-72, approximately 16 kilometres off the coast of Virginia Beach, two [F/A-18](/objects/fa-18) Super Hornets were split apart by a UAP.<sup>32</sup> It was described as a dark grey or black cube inside a clear sphere,<sup>33</sup> which came within approximately 15 metres of the lead aircraft<sup>34</sup> and was estimated to be roughly 1.5 to 4.5 metres in diameter.<sup>35</sup> The mission commander terminated the flight immediately and returned to base.<sup>36</sup> Navy Fighter Attack Squadron 11 submitted a safety report on the encounter, but there was no official acknowledgement of the incident and no further mechanism to report such sightings.<sup>37</sup> The object had no wings, emitted no infrared energy, and had nothing tethering it to the ground.<sup>38</sup> Pilots reporting UAP described a common characteristic of dark gray or black cubes inside a clear sphere, with the apex or tips of the cube touching the inside of the sphere,<sup>39</sup> and reports of this cube-in-sphere object occurred over almost eight years and, as far as Graves knew, were still occurring.<sup>40</sup>

During the Gimbal incident, the recording from the Advanced Technology FLIR system showed a single rotating object, while pilots could be heard referring to a fleet of objects that was not visible on the FLIR footage.<sup>41</sup> During a debrief, that fleet was shown to be part of the radar data displayed on the situational awareness page.<sup>42</sup> Graves saw a cluster of smaller craft flying in an inverted V-shape in front of the larger object on a longer version of the [Gimbal video](/documents/gimbal-video) he viewed aboard the USS Theodore Roosevelt.<sup>43</sup> He recalled that Rear Admiral Andrew L. Lewis, on viewing the video, said 'Hah!' and walked out.<sup>44</sup> The publicly released Gimbal/Go Fast Unidentified Aerial Phenomena / Unidentified Anomalous Phenomena (UAP) video was cut off at a point where the object appeared to rock slightly, showing some instability - the last that Graves saw of the footage.<sup>45</sup> He recognised the voices and video footage in the 2017 New York Times Unidentified Aerial Phenomena / Unidentified Anomalous Phenomena (UAP) report because he had personally seen the video when it was taken and when it was debriefed.<sup>46</sup>

Graves and other USS Theodore Roosevelt pilots were later summonsed to give confidential briefings to members of the [United States Congress](/organisations/united-states-congress) about the encounters.<sup>47</sup> His squadron had filed a safety report with the Navy after one incident but never heard back.<sup>48</sup> In July 2023, Congress held a bipartisan hearing on Unidentified Aerial Phenomena (UAP) at which Commander [David Fravor](/people/david-fravor), Lieutenant Ryan Graves and [David Charles Grusch](/people/david-charles-grusch) testified under oath.<sup>49</sup> There, Graves spoke on behalf of more than 30 commercial aircrew and military veterans who had confided similar encounters to him,<sup>50</sup> having personally experienced advanced UAP himself.<sup>51</sup> More than 30 witnesses have come forward to Americans for Safe Aerospace, and almost 5,000 Americans have joined its push for transparency;<sup>52</sup> most of these witnesses are commercial pilots at major airlines, often veterans with decades of flying experience.<sup>53</sup> Since the encounters, the Navy has implemented a Range Fouler Report to gather pilot information on UAP encounters on training ranges,<sup>54</sup> though a service-wide reporting mechanism for the US military remains pending implementation,<sup>55</sup> and since 2021 all UAP videos have been classified as secret or above.<sup>56</sup> Graves and Fravor have said they did not face repercussions from their superiors for reporting their experiences.<sup>57</sup> After the February 2023 unidentified-object shoot-downs, part of the 2023 'Balloongate' Unidentified Aerial Phenomena (UAP) shoot-down controversy, Graves warned that the White House should not conflate the low-tech objects shot down with the advanced objects witnessed by the pilots he served with.<sup>58</sup> He has worked with the American Institute of Aeronautics and Astronautics (AIAA) to help establish a UAP Integration Committee bringing engineering expertise to the study of Unidentified Anomalous Phenomena.<sup>59</sup> He has recommended utilising the existing Aviation Safety Reporting System (ASRS) as a short-term platform for pilots to report UAP,<sup>60</sup> which has built-in analytics capability and is funded by Congress.<sup>61</sup> Graves has said that since his initial East Coast encounters, UAP have been detected worldwide wherever the Navy conducts operations, according to the [All-domain Anomaly Resolution Office (AARO)](/projects/all-domain-anomaly-resolution-office-aaro).<sup>62</sup> He has also related, secondhand, an account of a 2003 incident at the 2003 Vandenberg Air Force Base Unidentified Aerial Phenomena / Unidentified Anomalous Phenomena (UAP) incident, in which a group of [Boeing](/organisations/boeing) contractors reported a large, roughly 90-metre red square object approaching the base from the ocean and hovering at low altitude over a launch facility;<sup>63</sup> according to Graves, it remained for about 45 seconds before darting off over the mountains.<sup>64</sup> Graves conceded there was a shocking possibility that the advanced UAP technology seen by his fellow pilots was deployed by China or another foreign adversary.<sup>65</sup> He was present backstage, alongside Lou Elizondo and members of Congress, at the 2024 United States congressional hearing on UAPs (13 November).<sup>66</sup>
