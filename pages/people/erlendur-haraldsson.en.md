Erlendur Haraldsson is an Icelandic psychologist and professor emeritus at the University of Iceland<sup>1</sup>, where he taught from 1973 to 1999<sup>2</sup> after completing a PhD in psychology at the University of Freiburg in 1972<sup>3</sup>. Since the 1970s he has researched apparitions, mediumship, and child reincarnation cases<sup>4</sup>. He was among a small group of researchers, alongside Ian Stevenson, Jürgen Keil, Satwant Pasricha, Antonia Mills and later Jim Tucker, who studied children reporting past-life memories in young children<sup>5</sup>; together they examined over twenty-five hundred such cases, identifying a matching previous person in roughly two-thirds<sup>6</sup>. Tucker reported that Haraldsson's follow-up work found a surprising number of these children still recalled past-life details as adults<sup>7</sup>.

A major strand of Haraldsson's work concerns after-death communication (ADC). In the mid-1970s he surveyed 902 Icelanders on whether they had ever sensed the presence of a deceased person<sup>8</sup>, an inquiry later published as The Departed Among the Living: An Investigative Study of Afterlife Encounters, in which thirty-one percent of respondents reported having had such an experience<sup>9</sup>. He went on to conduct in-depth interviews with over 450 people who described ADCs<sup>10</sup>, publishing findings including his paper "Alleged Encounters with the Dead: The Importance of Violent Death in 337 New Cases"<sup>11</sup>. According to this research, in 86 percent of cases occurring within twenty-four hours of death the percipient had not known the person had died<sup>12</sup>. About half of ADC events occurred within the first year after death, and three-quarters within four years<sup>13</sup>. He found that 28 percent of the deaths involved were sudden, unexpected or violent<sup>14</sup>, a rate far exceeding the 7.8 percent of violent deaths recorded in the general Icelandic population between 1941 and 1970<sup>15</sup>; he attributed the resulting male predominance among communicators to men being more likely to die violently than women<sup>16</sup>. He reported a similarly elevated proportion of violent deaths among apparition cases<sup>17</sup>, and stated that violent death features predominantly across apparitions, cases of the reincarnation type, and mediumship<sup>18</sup>, observing that such cases often have an invasive character since the deceased are frequently unknown to those who experience them<sup>19</sup>. He considered these findings, taken together, to support the survival hypothesis<sup>20</sup>, while concluding more broadly that it seemed impossible to reject all the accounts he collected as deception or mistaken perception<sup>9</sup>. Separately, in 1987 work with Loftur Gissurarson, the Anomalica record on this research states no association was found between high-scoring [Anomalous Cognition (AC)](/topics/anomalous-cognition-ac) trials and low Geomagnetic Fluctuations (GMF)<sup>21</sup>.

Haraldsson also examined drop-in communicators<sup>1</sup>, investigating, with Stevenson, the case involving Runolfur Runolfsson ("Runki") as it emerged through the mediumship of Hafsteinn Bjornsson<sup>22</sup>, a case previously described by Alan Gauld<sup>23</sup>. The two researchers judged it infeasible to attribute all the case's information to any single source<sup>24</sup>, and considered the simplest explanation to be that Runki had survived physical death and communicated through Bjornsson's mediumship<sup>25</sup>.

Haraldsson personally investigated in detail the physical mediumship of Indridi Indridason<sup>26</sup>, researching Indridason's work at the University of Iceland<sup>27</sup> and publishing on drop-in communicators connected to it over more than forty years<sup>28</sup>. Central to this work was the case of Emil Jensen, a communicator he considered comparable to the "Runki's leg" case described by Gauld<sup>29</sup>, and one he regarded, together with a case of independent voices singing in a language unknown to Indridason, as among the best evidence for human survival he had encountered<sup>30</sup>. He argued that telepathy or clairvoyance among living persons could not adequately explain the strongest drop-in cases<sup>31</sup>. His records indicate that the 63 Store Kongensgade fire, Copenhagen likely began after nine o'clock, eleven o'clock Danish time<sup>32</sup>. He did not closely examine the rediscovered Minute books of the Experimental Society (Iceland) until 2008, an event he described as perhaps the most memorable finding of his life<sup>33</sup>, noting that no attempt had previously been made to verify the Jensen communicator's biographical statements<sup>34</sup>. In 2009 he visited the Royal Library in Copenhagen, where he found annual volumes listing nineteenth-century businesspeople and, in the 1890 volume, a single manufacturer named Emil Jensen registered at Store Kongensgade 67, two doors from the fire at number 63<sup>35</sup>. Later that year he searched Copenhagen census records<sup>36</sup>, which showed Jensen living with four unmarried sisters at the same address<sup>37</sup>. He also checked the Copenhagen newspapers Politiken and Berlingske Tidende for an obituary of Jensen and found none<sup>38</sup>. All seven statements attributed to the Jensen communicator in the minute book of 1905-12-11 were subsequently verified<sup>39</sup>. Haraldsson concluded that identifying Jensen more than a century later confirmed him as an actual person, unknown to Indridason, who had died before appearing in Indridason's séance room<sup>40</sup>; author [Leslie Kean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leslie_Kean) similarly described Jensen as a drop-in communicator whose biographical details allowed him to be identified as someone who had genuinely lived<sup>41</sup>, an account documented in Emil Jensen's probate court document referenced in Haraldsson's research alongside the Death of Emil Jensen (1898).
