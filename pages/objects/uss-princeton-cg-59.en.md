USS Princeton (CG-59) is a [Ticonderoga-class](/objects/uss-nimitz-cvn-68) guided-missile cruiser serving in the United States Navy.<sup>1</sup> It was the first Ticonderoga-class cruiser to carry the upgraded [AN/SPY-1B radar](/objects/anspy-1-radar) system,<sup>2</sup> and operates two Seahawk LAMPS III helicopters.<sup>3</sup> In November 2004, Princeton was part of [Carrier Strike Group 11 (CSG-11)](/organisations/carrier-strike-group-11-csg-11), completing Tailored Ships Training Availability in preparation for deployment to the Arabian Sea.<sup>4</sup> The strike group comprised [USS Nimitz (CVN-68)](/objects/uss-nimitz-cvn-68), Princeton, USS Chafee (DDG-90), USS Higgins (DDG-76), and USS Louisville (SSN-724).<sup>5</sup> Princeton served as the strike group's primary air-defence platform, with its [AN/SPY-1](/objects/anspy-1) Aegis radar providing 360-degree phased-array coverage capable of tracking hundreds of targets simultaneously.<sup>6</sup>

Beginning on 2004-11-10, Princeton's radar operators detected what the official incident report designates as [anomalous aerial vehicles (AAVs)](/topics/anomalous-aerial-vehicle-anomalous-aerial-vehicle-aav) off the coast of California, initially observed flying at roughly 8,500 metres at approximately 190 kilometres per hour.<sup>7</sup> Over the following days, radar operators logged the activity repeatedly, with reports indicating over one hundred individual [UAP](/topics/unidentified-aerial-phenomena-uap) contacts in total before the main intercept.<sup>8</sup> The objects were detected on several separate occasions operating in and around the vicinity of the carrier strike group.<sup>9</sup> [Kevin Day](/people/kevin-day), a radar specialist aboard Princeton, verified the contacts using the ship's [Cooperative Engagement System](/objects/cooperative-engagement-system), which merged radar inputs from every available source into a single picture.<sup>10</sup> [Gary Voorhis](/people/gary-voorhis) also checked Princeton's radar system for false returns; the system was recalibrated and no technical faults were found.<sup>11</sup> The [AAVs](/objects/aav) were observed descending from above approximately 18,000 metres into the scan volume of the AN/SPY-1 radar,<sup>12</sup> then plummeting to as low as approximately 15 metres above the ocean surface,<sup>13</sup> with Day personally observing this descent occurring in approximately 0.78 of a second.<sup>14</sup>

A significant technical limitation affected Princeton's ability to track the objects. Because the AN/SPY-1 radar was configured for air intercept of conventional aircraft rather than ballistic missile tracking, it never obtained a stable track of the AAVs, dropping contacts that did not conform to expected parameters in order to reduce radar clutter.<sup>15</sup> The ship's senior chief, drawing on seventeen years of experience on Aegis cruisers, assessed that the AAVs exhibited characteristics consistent with ballistic missiles in their appearance, velocity, and radar indications.<sup>16</sup> Despite these limitations, five radar operators aboard Princeton tracked the [Tic Tac Sighting](/events/tic-tac-sighting) in real time during the eventual intercept.<sup>17</sup> The only units directly involved in the detection and intercept were Princeton, [Carrier Airborne Early Warning Squadron 117 (VAW-117)](/organisations/carrier-airborne-early-warning-squadron-117-vaw-117), [Marine Fighter Attack Squadron 232 (VMFA-232)](/organisations/marine-fighter-attack-squadron-232-vmfa-232), and [Strike Fighter Squadron 41 (VFA-41)](/organisations/strike-fighter-squadron-41-vfa-41).<sup>18</sup>

On 2004-11-14, Princeton took active control of the intercept operation. It took over direction of the [F/A-18](/objects/fa-18) flights from the [E-2 Hawkeye](/objects/e-2-hawkeye) airborne early warning aircraft and vectored them towards the AAV, leading to visual contact approximately 1.6 kilometres from the object.<sup>19</sup> On that morning, Princeton tasked two airborne [F/A-18F Super Hornets](/objects/fa-18f-super-hornet) already in the vicinity for reconnaissance of the contact.<sup>20</sup> [David Fravor](/people/david-fravor)'s flight launched from [USS Nimitz (CVN-68)](/objects/uss-nimitz-cvn-68) and Princeton's controller, using the callsign Poison, asked what ordnance they carried before redirecting them to real-world tasking.<sup>21,22</sup> During the intercept, situational awareness to the AAV was provided partly through sporadic [Link-16](/topics/link-16) data tracks from what was assumed to be Princeton's AN/SPY-1 radar.<sup>23</sup> Princeton was approximately 110 kilometres from the object at the time, placing it below the radar horizon for something as low as 12 metres above the water.<sup>24</sup> After Fravor's visual encounter concluded and the object disappeared, Princeton reacquired the target approximately 97 kilometres away<sup>25,26,27</sup> — Princeton had also radioed Fravor's flight while en route to their prearranged combat air patrol (CAP) point to advise that a UAP was already waiting for them at that position, a specific latitude and longitude that had not been transmitted on an open channel.<sup>28</sup>

Following the events of 2004-11-14, Princeton's commanding officer and the commanding officer of [Strike Fighter Squadron 41 (VFA-41)](/organisations/strike-fighter-squadron-41-vfa-41) reported all AAV activity to Vice Admiral [D. C. Curtis](/people/d-c-curtis).<sup>29</sup> Subsequent testimony raised questions about the handling of data from the encounter. Kevin Day reported that the morning after the intercept, he went to Princeton's communications room to retrieve radio recordings for an after-action report, but found all communications from the encounter had been deleted while date and time stamps remained intact.<sup>30</sup> Gary Voorhis stated he was ordered to hand over data tapes from Princeton's Combat Engagement Centre and, contrary to normal procedure, was also instructed to erase everything including blank tapes.<sup>31</sup> [David Fravor](/people/david-fravor) disputed a separate, widely circulated account alleging that personnel had physically boarded Princeton and USS Nimitz to confiscate and delete recordings, stating no such external collection of data occurred.<sup>32</sup>

The Princeton case has since been referenced as a point of comparison for later encounters. [Luis Elizondo](/people/luis-elizondo) assessed that the [2015 USS Roosevelt UAP Encounters](/events/2015-uss-roosevelt-uap-encounters) bore all the hallmarks of the Nimitz/Princeton case from 2004,<sup>33</sup> and the [2004 USS Nimitz UAP encounter](/events/2004-uss-nimitz-uap-encounter) as a whole — with Princeton as its central radar platform — remains one of the most extensively documented UAP incidents in the public record, drawing on official Navy reporting, congressional testimony, and first-hand accounts from personnel including [Kevin Day](/people/kevin-day), [Gary Voorhis](/people/gary-voorhis), and [David Fravor](/people/david-fravor). The [Tic Tac UAP Full Video](/documents/tic-tac-uap-full-video) captured during the intercept, along with testimony from [Douglas Kurth](/people/douglas-kurth) and others, has formed a substantial portion of the evidence base subsequently examined by researchers and government bodies investigating [unidentified aerial phenomena (UAP)](/topics/unidentified-aerial-phenomena-uap).
