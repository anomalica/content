USS Princeton (CG-59) is a Ticonderoga-class guided-missile cruiser of the United States Navy (USN)<sup>1</sup>, armed with naval guns and anti-air, anti-surface, and anti-submarine missiles<sup>2</sup>, and home to two Seahawk LAMPS III helicopters<sup>3</sup>. It was the first Ticonderoga-class cruiser to carry the upgraded AN/SPY-1 radar system<sup>4</sup>, later described by [David Fravor](/people/david-fravor) as a guided-missile cruiser with a helicopter pad rather than a fixed-wing flight deck<sup>6</sup>, fitted with a phased array radar of four panels each looking into a separate quadrant<sup>7</sup>. In November 2004 the ship was completing Tailored Ships Training Availability (TSTA) ahead of deployment to the Arabian Sea<sup>5</sup>, sailing as part of Carrier Strike Group 11 (CSG-11) alongside [USS Nimitz (CVN-68)](/objects/uss-nimitz-cvn-68), USS Chafee (DDG-90), USS Higgins and USS Louisville<sup>13</sup>. During the deployment, [Kevin Day](/people/kevin-day) served as the ship's Operations Specialist Senior Chief, responsible for coordinating the systems protecting the entire carrier group<sup>8</sup>.

In the days leading up to the [2004 USS Nimitz UAP encounter](/events/2004-uss-nimitz-uap-encounter), Kevin Day and his team grew increasingly concerned by anomalous radar tracks appearing on their screens<sup>9</sup>, which Day cross-checked using the Cooperative Engagement Capability (CEC) system that merged radar data from every source into a single picture<sup>10</sup>. Princeton was operating with its then-new SPY-1 Aegis radar system, providing air defence for the group in place of Nimitz itself<sup>15</sup>, using a passive electronically scanning array that gave 360-degree coverage of the carrier group<sup>16</sup>. The [Anomalous Aerial Vehicle (AAV)](/topics/anomalous-aerial-vehicle-aav) was detected by Princeton on three separate occasions during the week, operating off the western coasts of the United States and Mexico<sup>14</sup>. Because the AN/SPY-1 radar was set to a mode for air intercept of conventional aircraft, it never obtained an accurate track and repeatedly dropped the contacts as clutter<sup>17</sup>; had it been configured for ballistic-missile tracking it likely would have been able to track the vehicle<sup>18</sup>. The ship's Tactical Air Officer could not identify the contact and was perplexed by its speed and altitude<sup>19</sup>. According to "Imminent: Inside the Pentagon's Hunt for UFOs", radar operators logged more than one hundred such contacts around the vessels in the nearly two weeks before the encounter, performing manoeuvres the operators considered beyond any aircraft they had previously observed<sup>20</sup>.

On 2004-11-14, after again detecting the AAV, Princeton used a flight of two F/A-18F aircraft returning from a training mission to investigate further<sup>21</sup>, taking control from the E-2C Hawkeye and vectoring the jets to visual contact roughly 1.6 kilometres from the object<sup>22</sup>. Fravor's flight, callsign Blue Air, had been controlled by Princeton throughout the exercise<sup>23</sup>; the E-2C's controller had not seen the contact on his own radar until Princeton directed him and gave a general steer<sup>28</sup>, and the return was so low and faint that without Princeton's inputs it would likely have been missed or ignored<sup>29</sup>. Handed off from the E-2C with the callsign "Poison", Princeton vectored Fravor's flight via bearing, range and altitude calls towards an unknown contact approaching from the south<sup>24</sup>, at reported ranges of approximately 80, 64 and 48 kilometres<sup>27</sup>, and asked what ordnance the aircraft carried; the flight reported two captive training AIM-9M missiles and nothing else<sup>25</sup>. Fravor's weapon system operator assumed that the Link-16 data feeding their displays originated from Princeton's AN/SPY-1 radar<sup>26</sup>, and Fravor himself commanded the jet's Short Range radar set and requested a radar picture from the ship<sup>30</sup>. Princeton first reported the picture as clean, then reported that the object had climbed to Fravor's combat air patrol (CAP) station at approximately 7,300 metres<sup>31</sup>; Fravor later said the ship was too far away, at roughly 97 kilometres, for its radar to detect something near the ocean surface<sup>32</sup>.

After Fravor's crew engaged the object at low altitude, Princeton directed the flight to a prearranged rendezvous known as the "cap point", some 100 kilometres distant<sup>33</sup>; the coordinates for this point were never transmitted to the jets over an open radio channel<sup>34</sup>. As the flight neared the cap point, Princeton radioed that the object had reappeared there<sup>35</sup>. Elsewhere aboard the ship, Day observed a cluster of 14 unidentified objects on his radar screen that same late morning<sup>36</sup>; at the moment Fravor reported being engaged, those 14 other targets were also tracked plummeting from above approximately 24,000 metres towards the ocean<sup>37</sup>, and once the pilots returned to Nimitz, the objects shot back above roughly 24,000 metres in a fraction of a second<sup>38</sup>. Inside the Combat Information Centre, crew were tense as they listened to the increasingly stressed radio traffic, with five radar operators tracking the event in real time across the ship's sensor systems<sup>39</sup>.

Following the encounter, Day went to Princeton's communications room the next morning to obtain a copy of the previous day's radio traffic for an after-action report<sup>36</sup> but found that the recordings had been erased from the ship's optical disk, even though the date and time stamps remained<sup>37</sup>. Gary Voorhis was ordered to turn over the data tapes from the ship's Combat Engagement Centre<sup>40</sup> and separately checked the radar system for false returns, which was recalibrated with no problems found<sup>41</sup>. All of the activity surrounding the AAV was reported up the chain by the commanding officers of Princeton and [Strike Fighter Squadron 41 (VFA-41)](/organisations/strike-fighter-squadron-41-vfa-41) to D. C. Curtis<sup>42</sup>.
