The Nimitz UAP Incident, 2004 refers to a series of encounters between personnel of the [Nimitz Carrier Strike Group](/organisations/nimitz-carrier-strike-group) and unidentified aerial objects detected off the coast of California and Mexico during approximately 2004-11-10 to 2004-11-16.<sup>1</sup> The strike group was completing the Tailored Ships Training Availability in preparation for deployment to the Arabian Sea at the time of the encounters.<sup>1</sup> The [Nimitz Carrier Strike Group](/organisations/nimitz-carrier-strike-group) comprised [USS Nimitz](/objects/uss-nimitz) (CVN-68), [USS Princeton](/objects/uss-princeton) (CG-59), [USS Chafee](/objects/uss-chafee) (DDG-90), [USS Higgins](/objects/uss-higgins) (DDG-76), and [USS Louisville](/objects/uss-louisville) (SSN-724).<sup>5</sup> The squadrons involved in the actual detection and intercept of the Anomalous Aerial Vehicle (AAV) were the [USS Princeton](/objects/uss-princeton), [Carrier Airborne Early Warning Squadron 117 (VAW-117)](/organisations/carrier-airborne-early-warning-squadron-117-vaw-117), [Marine Fighter Attack Squadron 232 (VMFA-232)](/organisations/marine-fighter-attack-squadron-232-vmfa-232), and [Strike Fighter Squadron 41 (VFA-41)](/organisations/strike-fighter-squadron-41-vfa-41).<sup>6</sup>

On multiple occasions during the week, the [USS Princeton](/objects/uss-princeton) — the first Ticonderoga-class cruiser fitted with the upgraded [AN/SPY-1](/objects/anspy-1)B radar system<sup>7</sup> — detected AAVs descending from above approximately 18,000 metres at high velocity.<sup>8</sup> A senior fire control specialist aboard the Princeton, citing 17 years of experience on Aegis cruisers, assessed that the objects exhibited characteristics associated with ballistic missiles in terms of their appearance, velocity, and radar indications.<sup>9</sup> Because the Princeton's radar was configured for conventional aircraft intercept rather than ballistic missile tracking, it was never able to obtain an accurate track; the contacts were dropped by the radar as false targets to reduce clutter.<sup>10</sup> The AAVs were detected on three separate occasions during the week.<sup>11</sup> On 2004-11-14, after once again detecting an AAV, the Princeton tasked two airborne [F/A-18F](/objects/fa-18f) aircraft to conduct airborne reconnaissance of the contact.<sup>12</sup>

The first intercept on 2004-11-14 involved the commanding officer of [VMFA-232](/organisations/marine-fighter-attack-squadron-232-vmfa-232), who launched from [USS Nimitz](/objects/uss-nimitz) at approximately 1030 local time in a single-seat F/A-18C on a functional check flight.<sup>13</sup> As he descended through approximately 4,500 metres and approached within roughly 25 kilometres of the contact, he observed a circular water disturbance on the ocean surface, approximately 50 to 100 metres in diameter, with no visible object above, on, or below the surface.<sup>14</sup> Following the initial intercept, [David Fravor](/people/david-fravor) of [VFA-41](/organisations/strike-fighter-squadron-41-vfa-41) briefed the second flight, led by [Chad Underwood](/people/chad-underwood), in the paraloft and asked them to attempt to see and record what they could.<sup>15</sup> [Underwood](/people/chad-underwood) was the weapons and sensors officer (WSO) aboard the second [F/A-18F](/objects/fa-18f) section and launched shortly after the FASTEAGLE flight.<sup>15</sup>

[Chad Underwood](/people/chad-underwood) made multiple attempts to transition his aircraft radar to Single Target Track mode on the AAV, but the radar was unable to establish a lock, with the sweep rastering around the return, building an initial aspect vector that never stabilised, and then dropping.<sup>16</sup> Underwood noted there were no jamming cues — no strobe, champagne bubbles, or electronic attack indications — and that the radar simply appeared unable to receive sufficient information to create a target track file.<sup>17</sup> The forward-looking infrared (FLIR) system showed the object appearing stationary, with closure attributable only to the aircraft's own movement.<sup>18</sup> The AAV eventually moved out of Underwood's FLIR field of view; he made no attempt to slew the sensor and lost situational awareness of the contact.<sup>19</sup> Underwood subsequently stated that he could not confirm the FLIR contact was the same object as described by the FASTEAGLE flight, as he never had visual contact with it.<sup>20</sup> After landing, copies of Underwood's FLIR tapes were made and a set was turned in to the intelligence section.<sup>21</sup> The [Anomalous Aerial Vehicle (AAV) Forward Looking Infrared (FLIR) Recording 2004-11](/documents/anomalous-aerial-vehicle-aav-forward-looking-infrared-flir-recording-2004-11) is the footage associated with this event.

The [USS Louisville](/objects/uss-louisville), a Los Angeles-class nuclear fast attack submarine operating in the vicinity as part of the strike group,<sup>22</sup> reported no unidentified sonar contacts in the vicinity of the aerial sightings at any time during operations off California.<sup>23</sup> On this basis, the incident report assessed it as highly unlikely that any AAV operated below the ocean surface during the incident.<sup>24</sup> Following the intercept, [David Fravor](/people/david-fravor) reported to the [Carrier Intelligence Center (CVIC)](/organisations/carrier-intelligence-center-cvic) on 2004-11-14 to debrief; the Carrier Air Wing Intelligence Officer stated he was initially uncertain what to do with the report but had no reason to question it given Fravor's experience and standing.<sup>25</sup> An email-based Mission Report (MISREP), including the FLIR video, was submitted via a secure system to [3rd Fleet Intelligence](/organisations/3rd-fleet-intelligence).<sup>26</sup> The [3rd Fleet Intelligence](/organisations/3rd-fleet-intelligence) deputy confirmed the [Carrier Strike Group 11 (CSG-11) Anomalous Aerial Vehicle (AAV) Mission Report (MISREP) 2004-11](/documents/carrier-strike-group-11-csg-11-anomalous-aerial-vehicle-aav-mission-report-misrep-2004-11) was received on or about 2004-11-14 and was not forwarded further up the chain of command,<sup>27</sup> with the deputy noting it was likely deleted at some point thereafter as no requirement existed to retain such reports.<sup>28</sup>

All AAV activity was reported to the commander of the [Nimitz Carrier Strike Group](/organisations/nimitz-carrier-strike-group), Rear Admiral [D.C. Curtis](/people/dc-curtis), by the commanding officers of the [USS Princeton](/objects/uss-princeton) and [VFA-41](/organisations/strike-fighter-squadron-41-vfa-41).<sup>29</sup> The strike group's Director of Operations confirmed that the AAV was never considered a threat to the battle group, and that no advance knowledge of live-fire events, US weapons testing, or experimental aircraft in the area existed at the time.<sup>30</sup> The AAV was formally assessed as no known aircraft or air vehicle currently in the inventory of the United States or any foreign nation,<sup>31</sup> and was recorded as exhibiting advanced low-observable characteristics at multiple radar bands that rendered US radar-based engagement capabilities ineffective.<sup>32</sup> The objects further demonstrated advanced aerodynamic performance with no visible control surfaces and no visible means of generating lift.<sup>33</sup>
