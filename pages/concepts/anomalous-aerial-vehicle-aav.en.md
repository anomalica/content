The term Anomalous Aerial Vehicle (AAV) is used in US Navy incident documentation to designate an unidentified aerial object encountered by the [Nimitz Carrier Strike Group](/organisations/nimitz-carrier-strike-group) during the [2004 Nimitz UAP Incident](/matters/2004-nimitz-uap-incident). The designation appears most prominently in the *Nimitz Carrier Strike Group (CSG-11) AAV Incident Report*, which records observations, sensor data, and assessments gathered from multiple platforms and personnel during a series of detections and one confirmed visual intercept. The objects were detected on at least three separate occasions during the week while the strike group was operating off the western coast of the United States and Mexico.<sup>14</sup>

Prior to the visual intercept on 2004-11-14, the [USS Princeton](/objects/uss-princeton) had been tracking objects on its Aegis combat system for approximately two weeks.<sup>26</sup> According to [David Fravor](/people/david-fravor), the objects were observed descending from above approximately 24,000 metres rapidly to approximately 6,000 metres, remaining for hours, then ascending straight back up.<sup>27</sup> The [AN/SPY-1](/objects/anspy-1) radar aboard USS Princeton detected the AAV descending from above approximately 18,000 metres at high velocity into its scan volume.<sup>15</sup> Because the Princeton's radar was configured for air intercept of conventional aircraft rather than ballistic missile tracking, it never obtained an accurate track; the contacts were dropped from the system as apparent false targets.<sup>16</sup>

On 2004-11-14, after again detecting an AAV, the USS Princeton tasked two [F/A-18F](/objects/fa-18f) aircraft from [Strike Fighter Squadron 41 (VFA-41)](/organisations/strike-fighter-squadron-41-vfa-41) that were airborne in the vicinity to conduct airborne reconnaissance. The intercept took place at approximately N31°20' W117°10', approximately 130 kilometres south of the US/Mexico border and approximately 55 kilometres off the [Baja Mexico Coast](/places/baja-mexico-coast).<sup>17</sup> [David Fravor](/people/david-fravor) led the initial intercept flight, designated FASTEAGLE. On approach, Fravor's aircraft had no radar contacts and was carrying no forward-looking infrared (FLIR) pod.<sup>18</sup> Fravor and all four aircrew across the two aircraft visually observed a small white object below them, with its longitudinal axis oriented north–south, moving abruptly over a localised disturbance on the ocean surface.<sup>19</sup> The AAV was described consistently as shaped like an elongated egg or "Tic Tac", approximately 14 metres in length, solid white, smooth, with no edges, nacelles, pylons, or wings, and exhibiting no rotors, rotor wash, or visible flight control surfaces.<sup>19,28</sup>

When Fravor began a descent towards the AAV, the object appeared to reorient its axis in the direction of his aircraft, and the water disturbance ceased.<sup>20</sup> After Fravor manoeuvred approximately 90 degrees through a clockwise descending turn, the AAV climbed in a corresponding clockwise climbing turn, and when Fravor pulled his nose onto the object at approximately 460 metres' distance, the AAV rapidly accelerated and disappeared from view.<sup>20</sup> Both Fravor's aircraft and the wingman aircraft simultaneously lost visual contact.<sup>21</sup> The [USS Princeton](/objects/uss-princeton) subsequently reported via its [AN/SPY-1](/objects/anspy-1) radar that the AAV had reappeared at the flight's pre-assigned [Combat Air Patrol (CAP)](/concepts/combat-air-patrol-cap) point approximately 97 kilometres to the east.<sup>29</sup> Fravor assessed that the object had covered that distance in less than one minute.<sup>30</sup> A second intercept flight led by [Chad Underwood](/people/chad-underwood) was dispatched following the FASTEAGLE debrief in the paraloft. Underwood tracked the AAV via FLIR but was unable to obtain a radar lock; his [APG-73 Radar](/objects/apg-73-radar) repeatedly failed to transition to Single Target Track mode without displaying any conventional electronic attack indicators.<sup>22,23</sup> The resulting approximately 90-second FLIR recording became the [Navy F/A-18F Nimitz Encounter Video](/documents/navy-fa-18f-nimitz-encounter-video), which was released publicly in 2017. The targeting pod imagery showed that the AAV emitted no detectable infrared plume consistent with a conventional propulsion system.<sup>31</sup>

The *CSG-11 AAV Incident Report* recorded several key assessments. The AAV was assessed as no known aircraft or air vehicle in the inventory of the United States or any foreign nation.<sup>8</sup> It exhibited advanced low-observable characteristics across multiple radar bands, rendering US radar-based engagement capabilities ineffective.<sup>9</sup> No visible control surfaces or means of generating lift were observed.<sup>10</sup> Its propulsion appeared capable of maintaining a stationary hover before transitioning to velocities far exceeding any known aerial vehicle with little to no visible signature.<sup>11</sup> The report additionally noted that the AAV possibly demonstrated a capability to cloak or become invisible to the human eye<sup>12</sup> and possibly demonstrated the ability to operate undersea undetected by advanced US sonar systems.<sup>13</sup> However, based on the absence of any unidentified sonar contacts reported by the [USS Louisville](/objects/uss-louisville), a Los Angeles-class submarine operating as part of the strike group, the report assessed it as highly unlikely that the AAV had operated below the ocean surface.<sup>24</sup> The [Carrier Strike Group](/organisations/nimitz-carrier-strike-group) Director of Operations stated that at no time did they consider the AAV a threat to the battle group, and that they had no advance knowledge of any weapons testing or experimental aircraft activity in the area.<sup>25</sup> All AAV activity was reported to [D.C. Curtis](/people/dc-curtis) by the commanding officers of USS Princeton and [Strike Fighter Squadron 41 (VFA-41)](/organisations/strike-fighter-squadron-41-vfa-41).<sup>25</sup> In testimony to the [House Oversight Committee in 2023](/events/2023-fravor-house-oversight-committee-unidentified-aerial-phenomena-uap-testimony), Fravor stated that the AAV was far superior in performance to anything the United States had at the time, has today, or is projected to develop within the next ten or more years,<sup>32</sup> and that the incident was never officially investigated, that no crew members were formally questioned, and that no tapes were collected.<sup>33</sup>
